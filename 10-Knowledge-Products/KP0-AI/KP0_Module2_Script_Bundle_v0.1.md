## 3.1 Subtopic 2.1 — Build the context once

| Field | Value |

> **Single message —** _Write down once, with dates and sources, what every task needs to know about your country and your unit, and give each task only the part it needs._

### The concept

Every AI usage tip in this series begins 'Below is…'. It needs your material: your country's strategy, your sector's programmes, the laws that bind you. Without it, the assistant fills the gap from what it learned in training, which is general, undated and often wrong about your country. With it, the same prompt gives an answer about your own situation.

Most people build that material again for every task, a little differently each time. Build it once instead. Write a context pack: a few short sections that describe your country and your unit, each dated, each claim with its source. Then every task starts from the same checked facts. This is the same idea as a building block that a government plans once and every programme re-uses: the work is done once, and done properly, because many things depend on it.

More context is not better context. The engineers who build assistants call the discipline context engineering, and state its rule simply: give the assistant the smallest set of high-signal information that makes the result you want most likely. A pack of twenty pages pasted into a task that needs one section buries that section. The assistant gives each sentence some weight, so pages it does not need make the answer worse, not better. Keep the pack whole; give each task only its part.

A context pack goes stale. A strategy is approved, a body is renamed, a programme is cancelled. Date every section, and the pack tells you when it needs a second look.

### In the tool

Shown in Claude, and the same in any assistant that can search the web.

Building it. The research prompts of Play 0 build the country context pack, called A0 in the courses' workbooks, section by section: the digital landscape, the programmes, how the ministry works, the roles, the country's characteristics, the public bodies and systems, and the laws. Run them with web search on, one per chat, and ask for text in the chat, not a file.

Keeping it. Copy each answer into one document of your own, under its section heading, with the date you ran it. Open the sources on anything you will carry into a briefing, and mark any claim you could not check. Keep posts, not names: take out the names of office-holders before the pack is shared.

Using it. Paste the section a tip names, or attach the pack as a file and say which section the task uses. Module 2's next page shows how to keep the pack in a project, so that it no longer needs pasting.

### Worked example — Progressa's context pack, trimmed for one task

Progressa's context pack has seven sections. They are on the Progressa page of the courses, ready to paste. The data unit of MoEYS had a task that needed only one of them: draft the legal-basis paragraph of a data-sharing request to the examinations authority.

The first run pasted the whole pack. The paragraph that came back talked about connectivity, donors and the Digital Transformation Roadmap, and named the Data Protection Act only in passing. The second run pasted only section 7, the laws and policy, with its date. The paragraph named the two obligations of the Data Protection Act 2023 for a minor's data, a legal basis and parental consent for any use the law does not require, and noted that Progressa has no access-to-information act.

The tip below does the trimming. Run on this task, it kept section 7 and one sentence of section 3, the operating-model question, which says why the data is to be shared, and it flagged one thing as stale: section 7 does not say whether the Data Protection Commission has issued guidance since the pack was dated.

### AI usage tip — Trim a context pack to what one task needs

**What the prompt does:** A context pack is written once and is long; a task needs only part of it. Pasting the whole pack buries what matters, and choosing by hand each time is slow and misses things.

**Prompt template (copy-paste into the assistant):**

```text
Below is my context pack, dated [the date], in sections [paste the pack]. Below that is the task I am about to give an AI assistant [paste the task, or the prompt you will run]. Select only what the task needs. For each section, say: (a) whether the task needs it: all of it, part of it, or none; (b) if part, the sentences to keep, quoted word for word; (c) one line on why. Then list anything the task needs that the pack does not hold, and anything in the kept part that may be out of date, with the reason. Do not rewrite or summarise the kept sentences. Output: the trimmed pack, as the kept sentences under their section headings; then the table of sections; then the two lists.
```

**Inputs and outputs:** Input: your context pack and the task. Output: the trimmed pack, the sections dropped with the reason, and what the pack lacks or may have let go stale.

**Safeguard:** The assistant can drop a constraint the task needs without saying so: a law, a limit on data, a body that must agree. Before you use the trimmed pack, read the list of dropped sections and put back anything that binds the task.

## 3.2 Subtopic 2.2 — Projects and standing instructions

| Field | Value |

> **Single message —** _Say once, in a project, what you would otherwise paste into every chat: who you are, what you produce, the rules, and the files._

### The concept

A unit that uses an assistant every day starts most chats the same way. It says who it is, what it is working on, which rules apply, and pastes the same files. That is work done again and again, and done a little differently each time, so the answers differ too.

Say it once. Most assistants let you set up a project: a place that holds standing instructions and a set of files, which every chat started in it can see. The instructions are read at the start of every chat in the project, before your first message. The files are there to be read when a chat needs them.

Write the standing instructions as a project brief, the way you would brief a new member of staff. Who the unit is, and the post that owns the project. What the project is for. What the unit produces, and in what format. The standing rules: posts, not names; cite or discard; text in the chat, not files; open questions listed at the end of every draft. What each file is, and its date. What never goes in. And who reviews the brief, and when.

A brief does two things. It makes every chat in the project start from the same place, so answers can be compared. And it writes down the unit's intent, the goals and limits that otherwise live only in people's heads. When the person who set the project up moves on, the brief stays.

Teams that use assistants to write software keep the same kind of brief in a file the assistant reads first, such as one named CLAUDE.md or AGENTS.md. The idea is the same; only the place differs.

### In the tool

Shown in Claude.

Setting up a project. Create a project, give it a name, and paste the brief into its instructions. Add the files: the context pack, the strategy, the template the unit writes to. Then start every chat on that work from inside the project.

Keeping it current. Change the brief when the work changes, and replace a file when a newer version is approved, rather than adding the new one beside the old. Two versions of the same document in one project give two different answers.

Sharing it. In an organisation's account, a project can be shared with colleagues, who then work from the same brief and files. Share it only with the people the files may be shown to.

The blank project brief below is the form to fill. In ChatGPT the same thing is called a project, in Gemini a Gem, and in Microsoft 365 Copilot a notebook: see the reference page of this course, Assistants compared.

| Part of the brief | What to write |
| --- | --- |
| Who we are | The unit, its body, and the post that owns this project |
| What this project is for | The work, in one or two sentences, and who it is for |
| What we produce | The outputs, their format, their length and their reader |
| Standing rules | Posts, not names. Cite or discard. Text in the chat, not files. Open questions listed at the end of every draft. Any rule of your own |
| The files | Each file in the project: what it is, its date, and who approved it |
| What never goes in | The kinds of material that must stay out of this project, by name |
| Review | The post that reviews this brief, and the date of the next review |

### Worked example — MoEYS's project brief

The data unit of MoEYS set up a project for the National Learner Registry. Its brief, in full:

Who we are: the data unit of the Ministry of Education, Youth and Skills of Progressa; the project is owned by the head of the data unit. What this project is for: preparing the papers for the National Learner Registry, the single list of learners the Education Sector Plan 2023–2028 calls for, for the Permanent Secretary and the Registry's steering group. What we produce: briefing notes of one page, data-sharing requests, and gap tables; plain English; tables with named columns. Standing rules: posts, not names; every claim about a law, a body or a country with its source, or it is dropped; text in the chat; every draft ends with its open questions. The files: Progressa's country context pack, dated 12 October 2026; the Education Sector Plan 2023–2028; the Data Protection Act 2023. What never goes in: records of learners, even a sample; unpublished cabinet papers; the names of office-holders. Review: the head of the data unit, on the first working day of each quarter.

The first chat in the project asked for a one-page note on the Registry's legal basis. It came back with a table and four open questions, with no names, without being told any of it in the chat.

### AI usage tip — Draft the standing instructions for your team's project

**What the prompt does:** A team knows how it works but has never written it down for an assistant, so every chat starts again from nothing, and each colleague starts differently.

**Prompt template (copy-paste into the assistant):**

```text
Below is a description of my unit and its work with an AI assistant: who we are, what we produce and for whom, the rules we follow, and the documents we use [paste the description]. Draft the standing instructions for a project our team will share, under these seven headings: who we are; what this project is for; what we produce; standing rules; the files; what never goes in; review. Under 'standing rules', always include: posts, not names; cite or discard; text in the chat, not files; open questions listed at the end of every draft. Under 'the files', list each document I named with a blank for its date and approver. Use only what my description says; where it says nothing for a heading, write 'to be decided' and the question to answer. Output: the brief, under the seven headings, in no more than 400 words, then a list of the points marked 'to be decided'.
```

**Inputs and outputs:** Input: a description of your unit and its work. Output: the standing instructions for your team's project under seven headings, with the points still to be decided.

**Safeguard:** Standing instructions are read by every chat in the project, so a wrong rule is repeated in every answer. The head of the unit reads the brief line by line before it goes into the project, and fills each blank date and approver from the documents themselves.

> **Single message —** _A skill packages a procedure you repeat; a connector lets the assistant reach a source; choose the lightest setup that does the job._

### The concept

A project carries what an assistant should know. Two other things change what it can do.

A skill is a procedure packaged with what it needs: the steps, the reference material, and the shape of the output. It sits ready, and the assistant loads it when a task calls for it, instead of you pasting the procedure each time. Each skill has a short header that says what it is for; the assistant reads only the headers until one matches the task, so many skills cost almost nothing until one is used. Skills follow an open standard, Agent Skills, so the same skill can run in more than one assistant.

A connector lets the assistant reach a source: a document library, a knowledge base, a register. With a connector, the assistant reads the current version of the source when it needs it, instead of a copy you pasted last month. Most connectors follow one open protocol, the Model Context Protocol, MCP for short.

Choose the lightest setup that does the job. A task you do once is a prompt. Context the unit's work always needs goes in a project. A procedure you repeat the same way, ten times a month, is a skill. A source that changes, and must be read as it is today, is a connector. Each step up is more work to set up and more to keep current, so step up only when the lighter one is no longer enough.

This series already offers skills. The optional ea-plays kit packages the procedures of some AI usage tips as skills. They bring the named source in before the draft is written, and check the draft after: every output opens with a header that counts the sources by kind and the lines nobody could verify. The tips run without the kit; the kit makes the step most people skip harder to skip.

### In the tool

Shown in Claude.

Installing one skill. Download the ea-plays kit from its public repository as a zip file, and upload the folder of one skill in the settings, under skills. Each skill folder is complete on its own. Then open the skill's main file and read its header: the name and the one-paragraph description are what the assistant reads to decide when to use it. If the description does not match how your unit would ask, the skill will not be used when you expect.

Connecting the course's knowledge base. The courses are published on GitBook, and a published GitBook site offers a connector of its own: the site's address followed by /~gitbook/mcp. Add it in the settings as a custom connector. The assistant can then search and read the courses' pages as they are published today. The connector can only read.

Turning them off. Skills and connectors can be switched off in the settings, or for one chat in the tools menu. Switch off what a task does not need: fewer tools, like less context, gives a better answer.

### Worked example — A reference check, packaged and connected

The architecture unit of PDGA checks its papers against PAERA, GovStack's reference architecture for public administration, by section, ten or more times a month. Each analyst did it differently, and one paper cited a section that PAERA does not have.

The unit installed one skill of the ea-plays kit, the PAERA reference check, and connected the courses' knowledge base. The next paper, a note on Progressa's data-exchange gaps, came back with the header the kit puts on every output: nine sources, seven of them primary; two lines unverified. The two lines named PAERA sections. The skill had looked each one up at its public address; one existed, one did not, and the header said so instead of hiding it.

The unit kept the project for its standing context, the skill for the check, and the connector for the courses. A one-off question about a meeting stayed a prompt.

### AI usage tip — Decide which tasks need a prompt, a project, a skill or a connector

**What the prompt does:** A unit uses an assistant for many tasks, set up anyhow. Some are pasted again every week; some reach for a source that has changed. Nobody has decided which setup each task deserves.

**Prompt template (copy-paste into the assistant):**

```text
Below is a list of the tasks my unit does with an AI assistant, with how often each is done and what it uses [paste the list]. For each task, recommend one of four setups: (1) a prompt, for a task done once or rarely; (2) a project, for context the unit's work always needs; (3) a skill, for a procedure repeated the same way; (4) a connector, for a source that changes and must be read as it is today. For each task produce one row with: (a) the task; (b) the setup; (c) the reason, in one line, from how often it is done, whether its steps repeat, and whether its source changes; (d) what the setup would hold. Recommend the lightest setup that does the job. Where a task would put personal or confidential data into a project, skill or connector, say so and recommend a prompt with placeholders instead. Output: a table with one row per task, then the setups in the order to build them.
```

**Inputs and outputs:** Input: the list of the unit's tasks with an assistant, with how often each is done and what it uses. Output: a table of which tasks need a prompt, a project, a skill or a connector, with the reason for each and the order to build them.

**Safeguard:** A connector lets the assistant read a source in every chat that uses it, and what it reads can be repeated in any answer. Agree each connector with the owner of the source before you add it, whatever the table recommends.

## 3.4 Subtopic 2.4 — Judgement: what never goes into the setup

| Field | Value |

> **Single message —** _A setup is used by every later chat, so nothing confidential goes into it, every file carries a date, and the intent is written down._

### The concept

What goes into one chat is used once. What goes into a project, a memory, a skill or a connector is used by every later chat, and often by every colleague the setup is shared with. So the safeguards of Module 1 bind harder here.

Nothing confidential goes into a setup. No records of citizens or learners, not even a sample. No unpublished cabinet papers. No names of office-holders: posts, not names. A connector to a source that holds such data puts it within reach of every chat that uses the connector. And an assistant that remembers across chats can carry a detail from one conversation into another.

Context goes stale. A file added in March is still read in October, and the assistant cannot tell that it has been replaced. Date every file, replace rather than add, and review the setup on a date written in the brief.

Write the intent down. The researcher Margaret-Anne Storey names three kinds of debt that work with assistants piles up. Technical debt lives in the code. Cognitive debt lives in people: work accepted that nobody in the team can explain. Intent debt lives in the documents: the goals and limits that were never written down, so neither the next colleague nor the assistant can follow them. A project brief that says what the unit is for, what it must never do and why, pays off intent debt. A setup without one runs on what the person who made it remembered.

### In the tool

Shown in Claude.

Checking a project. Open the project and read its instructions and its list of files, as a stranger would. For each file, ask what it is, its date, and whether it may be shown to everyone the project is shared with.

Checking memory. If memory is on, the settings show what the assistant has kept about you from earlier chats. Read it, correct what is wrong, and delete what should not be kept. For a chat that must leave nothing behind, use an incognito chat, which is not saved and not remembered.

Checking connectors. The settings list the connectors in use. Remove any that no current task needs.

### Worked example — A project audited

On the first working day of the quarter, the head of the data unit of MoEYS audited the National Learner Registry project, as its brief requires. The tip below read the brief and the list of files.

It found three things. The roles register was last year's, and named two office-holders: it was replaced by the current one, by post. There were two versions of the Education Sector Plan, the 2023 plan and a 2025 draft revision, and some answers had quoted the draft as if it were in force: the draft was removed. And the brief never said why the Registry needs parental consent, so drafts kept treating consent as optional: one line was added under standing rules, with the section of the Data Protection Act 2023 it comes from.

Memory held one item worth removing: the name of a head teacher mentioned in an earlier chat. The head of the unit deleted it, and recorded the audit and its date at the foot of the brief.

### AI usage tip — Audit what a project holds

**What the prompt does:** A project grows by addition. Files are added and rarely removed, and the brief drifts from the work. Nobody checks what every chat in it is now reading, or whether it should.

**Prompt template (copy-paste into the assistant):**

```text
Below are the standing instructions of a project my team shares in an AI assistant [paste the instructions], and the list of its files, each with its title and date [paste the list]. Audit the project. For the instructions and for each file, produce one row with: (a) the item; (b) its date, or 'no date'; (c) any risk: personal data or names, unpublished or confidential material, out of date, contradicted by another item, or two versions of the same document; (d) the action: keep, replace, remove, or ask the owner. Then list the goals, limits and reasons the work depends on that the instructions do not state, as lines to add. Judge only the titles, dates and text given; where a risk can only be seen in a file's content, say 'read the file'. Output: the table, then the lines to add, then the number of items marked for each action.
```

**Inputs and outputs:** Input: the project's standing instructions and the list of its files with titles and dates. Output: an audit of what the project holds, with an action for each item and the lines of intent to add.

**Safeguard:** The assistant sees only the titles and the text you paste, not what is inside each file: a file with a harmless title can hold names or confidential material. The owner of each file reads it before it is marked 'keep'.

### Script (on camera, slides cut in)

> _Opening: the speaker on camera. The title is in the lower third: 'Context once, then skills'._

For the first weeks of writing these courses with an AI assistant, every chat began the same way. We pasted the country, the ministry, the rules. Every chat began slightly differently, and so every answer did too.

> **The speaker's story —** The speaker's own case, 60 to 90 seconds, about 150 to 200 words. It must show three things: (1) building the country context pack once, for The Gambia or for Progressa, and what changed in every AI usage tip run after it: answers that could be compared, sources that had already been checked; (2) a step the test learners skipped, opening the sources, and why the ea-plays skills were written to make that step hard to skip; (3) one thing the speaker would set up differently now. Give the date. Name posts, not persons. If the speaker cannot name a case with a date, this video becomes a page (plan decision 4).

> _Slide cut in — Title: 'Four setups, lightest first'. Body, four text rows: 'Once: a prompt.' 'Always needed: a project.' 'Repeated the same way: a skill.' 'A source that changes: a connector.'_

Here is the rule we work by now. A task you do once is a prompt. Context your work always needs goes into a project, with a brief: who we are, what we produce, the rules, and the files, each with its date. A procedure you repeat the same way becomes a skill. And a source that changes is reached through a connector, so the assistant reads it as it is today. Step up only when the lighter setup is no longer enough.

> _Slide cut in — Title: 'Less is better'. Body, two text rows: 'Give each task the smallest set of information it needs.' 'A pack of twenty pages buries the one section a task uses.'_

And give each task only what it needs. We learned this the slow way. Pasting the whole context pack into every task felt thorough. It made the answers worse, because the one section that mattered was buried under nineteen that did not.

> _Back to the speaker on camera._

One warning. Everything in a setup is read by every chat that uses it, and often by every colleague it is shared with. So no records of real people, ever. No unpublished papers. Every file with its date. And write down why the work is done the way it is, because the person who set it up will not always be there to explain.

> _Slide cut in — Title: 'In one sentence'. Body, large text (Arial Bold 28pt): 'Write the context once and the procedure once; then every chat starts from them.'_

Write the context once, and the procedure once. Then every chat starts from them.

## 4. Production notes

### 4.1 Design standard

As in Module 1: a public servant with the page on one half of the screen and an assistant on the other must be able to run the page's AI usage tip on their own work and judge its result. For Module 2, the result is a setup: a trimmed context pack, a project brief, a decision on which setup each task needs, or an audit.

### 4.2 The video

V2 is one person on camera, with three text-only slides cut in on the ITU template: title Arial Bold 28pt, body Arial 18pt, background #E5F5FB. It is recorded by the camera route of the series. The story beat is the speaker's; the four setups and the single message are said as written.

### 4.3 Voice and tone

Direct address. Plain language at about an eighth-grade English level. Two technical names cannot be avoided, because learners will meet them in their assistant's settings: a skill and a connector, with MCP named once. Each is explained in plain words where it first appears.

### 4.4 The kit

Page 2.3 names the optional ea-plays kit and shows one of its skills. The tips of every page run without it. The page says how to install one skill in the Claude app; the kit's own page in the GEA space carries the other routes.

### 4.5 The reference table

Assistants compared (section 3.6) is a KP0 reference page, linked from every page whose In the tool names a setting. It is carried in this bundle because most of its rows are Module 2's. When the GitBook guide is rendered it becomes its own page.
