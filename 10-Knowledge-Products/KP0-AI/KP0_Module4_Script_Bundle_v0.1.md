## 3.1 Subtopic 4.1 — The log: status is produced, never remembered

> **Single message —** _Write one row for every event, and answer 'where does it stand?' from the log, never from memory._

### Try it first — The parallel mistake

On 30 October 2026, four pieces of work on the registry prototype were ready to start, each building one thing the prototype lets a user do: enrol a learner, move a learner to another school, register an examination candidate, and show the planning unit's figures. The chat in the orchestrator's role recommended running all four at once to save time, and the owner agreed.

The prototype was run with a plugin that starts each work chat on a timer (Module 5 shows such a plugin). Each piece of work was also started by hand, and for two of the four the timer fired as well, so they ran twice. All four were writing in one shared folder. Two copies of the same work, and four pieces of work, were changing the same files at the same time.

The owner withdrew all four and had them run again, one after another. Each was then accepted. The withdrawn rows stayed in the log, with the reason.

**Your question:** Read before each start, what in the log would have stopped the clash?

**Answer key:**

Before the second start, a row saying 'instruction issued' with no 'report back' after it: a piece of work already open. Before the fourth, three such rows. A log read before each start shows what is already running. With the rule 'one piece of work writes at a time', the other three would not have started at all, and the double starts would have had nothing to collide with. In the log, the same version issued twice is the sign of a double start.

The lesson the record took: one piece of work writes at a time. Running in parallel saves minutes, and costs the whole round when two of them touch the same files.

### The concept

A log is one row per event. A piece of work is issued; its report comes back; its review comes back with a verdict; you rule on it. Each event is a row, with seven columns: the date, the piece of work, its version, the event, the file, the verdict or ruling, and who it is open with. The event is one of five words: instruction issued, report back, review back, my ruling, handover. A ruling is accepted, not accepted or withdrawn. Nothing else goes in.

When anyone asks where the work stands, answer from the log, never from memory. A figure from memory is repeated as fact by people who were not there. A figure from the log can be produced again by anyone, and gives the same answer.

Nothing is deleted. A withdrawn piece of work keeps its rows, with the reason. That way the log counts what really happened: the registry prototype closed 23 pieces of work in its first week, 26 to 30 October 2026: 13 accepted at the first version and 10 that needed a second attempt. Of those 10, 4 were withdrawn and run again, 2 were closed by changing what was asked, and 4 were accepted at version 2. Expect nearly half of your work to need a second attempt. That is not failure; it is the review doing its job.

One piece of work writes at a time. Two chats changing the same files at once is the one mistake no review can repair: nothing in that round can be trusted, and all of it must be run again.

### In the tool

By hand, in the log you started on page 3.2. One piece of work is not enough to run the cycle for days, so start two more now: two pieces of your own work that meet one of the five conditions of 3.1, each with its instruction from the form. Run them over the next week or two, one writing at a time, beside the pages of this module. By 4.3 your log holds three pieces of work.

Write the row before you move on, not at the end of the day. Keep the log open beside the chats. A withdrawn piece of work gets a 'my ruling' row that says withdrawn and why; its earlier rows stay.

To answer 'where does it stand?', filter the log by piece of work and read its last row. To count the week, count the 'my ruling' rows: accepted, not accepted, withdrawn.

### AI usage tip — Answer 'where does it stand?' from the log alone

**What the prompt does:** When you answer status questions from memory, you round off, forget the withdrawn work and remember the good news. The log does not.

**Prompt template (copy-paste into the assistant):**

```text
Below is my work log, one row per event [paste the log, or attach it]. Use only the log; add nothing from anywhere else. For each piece of work, give: its latest version, its last event and date, whether it is open or closed, and, if open, who it is open with. Then give the totals: pieces of work closed, accepted at the first version, accepted after a second attempt, not accepted, and withdrawn. Then list anything in the log that looks wrong: two starts of the same version with no report between them, a verdict with no review row, a ruling with no verdict, a new version with no ruling before it. Quote the rows. Output: the table, the totals, then the list.
```

**Inputs and outputs:** Input: your log. Output: where each piece of work stands, the week's totals, and the rows that look wrong.

**Safeguard:** Compare the answer with what you remembered before you ran it. Where they differ, the log is the record; where the log is wrong, fix it with a new row that says why, never by deleting the old one.

**Time:** ~10 min · **Watch for:** An answer that adds what the log does not say.

**Practise on Progressa — input:**

A log of MoEYS's planning unit: '28 Oct, census count, v1, instruction issued. 28 Oct, census count, v1, report back. 29 Oct, census count, v1, review back, not accepted. 29 Oct, census count, v2, instruction issued. 29 Oct, school map, v1, instruction issued. 29 Oct, school map, v1, instruction issued. 30 Oct, census count, v2, report back.'

**Practise on Progressa — answer key:**

Census count: version 2, report back on 30 October, open, waiting for its review. School map: version 1, issued twice on 29 October with no report: a possible double start, to check before anything else runs. Census count again: version 2 was issued on 29 October with no 'my ruling' row after the review came back not accepted, so the decision to try again is not on record; add the ruling with its date. Totals: nothing closed yet. A good run names both; a weak one counts the school map once and moves on.

**With the kit:** the orchestrator plugin (in preparation) writes the log itself, one entry per event, and answers 'where does it stand?' from it whenever you ask. It cannot stop two pieces of work you start yourself from colliding: one writes at a time is still your rule. Optional: the cycle runs by hand.

> **Single message —** _Keep a page of faults for each kind of work, and add every fault a review finds, so that every later instruction carries it._

### Try it first — Where the drafting page's line came from

Module 3 ended with the note to the Permanent Secretary. Its review found two sentences that stated a limit of the registry's search as a limit of the whole prototype: 'it does not recognise a child already registered under a different spelling'.

After that review, the page of faults for drafting gained a line. It now says two things: every limit of the thing described that an accepted document names is either in the draft or in the list of what was left out; and 'a limit of one step is stated of that step, not of the whole'. Every drafting instruction written after that day carries the line.

**Your question:** Think of a review of your own work, in Module 3 or before it. Which finding would have added a line to your page?

**Answer key:**

There is no single answer. A good line names the fault, not the instance: not 'the census paragraph mixed two counts', but 'define each figure before counting it, and give figures that measure different things separately'.

A line belongs on the page when the fault could recur in other work of the same kind. A fault that belongs to one task only goes into that task's instruction, under how this work can fail.

### The concept

Each kind of work fails in its own ways. Analysis tends to lean towards one position, or to count over a set it never listed. Drafting tends to supply what the source does not carry, or to say more than the record does. Checking tends to trust a clean result from a check that was never seen to fail.

So keep one page of faults for each kind of work you do. Each page says what the work chat refuses to do, what the review chat runs before it judges, and what you check before you issue an instruction of that kind. The starter folder has four pages to begin with: analysis, drafting, checking and building.

The pages grow. When a review finds a fault the page does not yet carry, add it to the page in the same sitting. Every later instruction of that kind copies the page's faults into its section on how the work can fail, so you learn each fault once and never pay for it again.

Birgitta Böckeler sorts what you put around an assistant into two kinds: guides, which steer it before it works, and sensors, which check what it did so that it can be corrected. A page of faults is both. What the work chat refuses is a guide; what the review chat runs is a sensor. Each needs the other. A guide that is never checked never finds out whether it worked, and a check with no guide catches the same fault again and again. That is why a fault the review finds goes back onto the page.

A correction goes where the fact lives. If a review shows that a figure in your context pack (the dated facts about your country and unit, from page 2.1) is wrong, correct the pack, not the instruction that quoted it. Otherwise the next instruction quotes the same wrong figure.

### In the tool

By hand, in any assistant. Copy the page for the kind of work you do most from the starter folder's kinds folder into your own folder, and keep it there.

When you write an instruction, copy the page's faults into its section 6, then add the two or three ways particular to the task.

When a review comes back, read its gaps against the page. A gap the page already carries goes into version 2. A gap the page does not carry goes into version 2 and onto the page, written as a rule.

### AI usage tip — Turn a review's finding into a line on your page

**What the prompt does:** A fault written as the instance ('the census paragraph mixed two counts') will not be recognised the next time. Written as a rule, it is caught in every later instruction.

**Prompt template (copy-paste into the assistant):**

```text
Below is my page of faults for one kind of work [paste the page] and a review of one piece of that kind of work [paste the review]. For each gap the review names: (a) say whether the page already carries it, and quote the line if it does; (b) if it does not, write one new line for the page, as a rule that would apply to any piece of this kind of work, not only to this one; (c) say whether the gap is particular to this task, in which case it belongs only in the task's instruction. Do not rewrite the existing lines. Output: a table of the gaps, then the new lines, ready to paste.
```

**Inputs and outputs:** Input: your page of faults and one review. Output: each gap matched to the page, and new lines written as rules.

**Safeguard:** A new line that is too wide stops good work; one that is too narrow catches nothing. Read each one against two other pieces of work of the same kind before you add it.

**Time:** ~10 min · **Watch for:** A line that names the instance, not the rule.

**Practise on Progressa — input:**

The census review from Module 3: 'Gap 1: the figure 37 counts schools with no return and schools with an empty enrolment field as one, without saying so.' The starter folder's page of faults for checking.

**Practise on Progressa — answer key:**

The page carries 'list the set first from its own source', which is close but not the same. A good new line: 'Define each figure before counting it; where two different things could be counted under one word, count them separately and say so.' It applies to every count, not only the census.

> **Single message —** _Hand over only what the files cannot say, as claims someone can check, and make the next chat's first task to check a few of them and stop._

### Try it first — The confessions

On 30 October 2026, the chat that had held the orchestrator's role on the registry prototype for four days was retired, and a fresh chat took over. The outgoing chat wrote a handover note. Its first section was headed 'What this session did that it should not have done'. Some of its lines, rewritten plainly:

It edited the project's main documents itself, on the owner's rulings, where a piece of work should have done it. It started a review before the work it reviewed. It designed the four pieces of work to run at the same time in one folder, with no guard against a timer starting them a second time. It stopped a program still running in the background after the work was withdrawn.

The note ended with four claims for the fresh chat to check first, each with where to check it. The fresh chat checked all four, and all four held. The note's other claims were left unchecked.

**Your question:** Which one claim would you have added to the four?

**Answer key:**

A strong candidate: 'No piece of work is running twice.' It follows from the parallel mistake, it can be checked against the log in a minute, and if it were false every other claim would be in doubt.

A good claim is one whose failure would change what the next chat does first, and that can be checked against a named file or row. 'The work is in good shape' is not a claim; 'the four pieces of work of 4.1 are each accepted at version 2' is.

### The concept

A chat that runs for days starts to quote figures it no longer works out again. That is the second failure of Module 3 coming back: what a chat knows lives in the chat. The answer is to retire the chat and start a fresh one, which reads the files instead of remembering.

The handover note carries only what the files cannot: what the outgoing chat did that it should not have done; what is owed, to whom, and by when; and findings that appear in no report. It refuses figures, which belong in the files where they can be worked out again; a survey of the work, which the log already gives; and a story, which nobody can check. Every line is a claim.

The fresh chat's first task is small: check a handful of the claims against the files, then stop and wait. Keep the list short. A list of everything is a list nobody checks.

The same note serves when you hand the work to a colleague, or come back to it yourself after a holiday.

### In the tool

By hand, in any assistant. Write the note from the starter folder's handover form, in your own words or with the outgoing chat's help. Save it beside the log.

Open a new chat with memory off. Paste the first-task prompt, the note, the log, and every file a claim of the note names, such as a report or a review: the fresh chat can check a claim only against what it has been given. Read its answer: each claim marked holds or does not hold, with what it looked at. If it went further than checking, stop it: it was told to stop.

Write a row in the log: 'handover', with the note's file name.

### AI usage tip — Write the handover note for your work

**What the prompt does:** A handover written as a story feels complete and cannot be checked. A note of claims can be checked in minutes, and shows the next person where to start.

**Prompt template (copy-paste into the assistant):**

```text
Below are my work log [paste it] and my notes on the work so far [paste them]. Write a handover note with three sections and a list, and nothing else. (1) What I did that I should not have done: steps out of order, decisions taken that were not mine, shortcuts. (2) What is owed: what is open, to whom, by when. (3) Findings that appear in no report. Then: the claims to check first, three to five, each one a single sentence that can be checked against a named file or log row, with where to check it. Leave out every figure, any survey of the work and any story. Where my notes do not say something, leave the line out rather than guess.
```

**Inputs and outputs:** Input: your log and your notes. Output: a handover note of claims, and three to five claims for the next chat to check first.

**Safeguard:** Only you know what you did that you should not have done; the chat can only find it in what you give it. Read section 1 and add what is missing before you hand over.

**Time:** ~15 min · **Watch for:** A note that retells the work instead of claiming.

**Practise on Progressa — input:**

The census log from 4.1, and these notes: 'Version 2 of the census count is waiting for its review. I started the school map twice by mistake on 29 October and did not check whether both ran. The director wants the census figure by 5 November. I told the director informally it would be about 30.'

**Practise on Progressa — answer key:**

Section 1: the school map started twice and not checked; a figure given to the director informally before it was reviewed. Section 2: the census figure, owed to the director by 5 November. Claims: 'the census count version 2 has a report and no review yet' (the log); 'only one school map chat ran' (the log and the reports folder). A good run leaves out 'about 30': it is a figure, and an unreviewed one.

### Try it first — Seven mixed-up moments

Each moment below breaks one rule of this module. Name the rule.

| # | The moment |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | The first piece of work is still writing in the folder, and you start the second in the same folder to save time. |
| 2 | A piece of work is withdrawn, and you delete its rows so that the log looks clean. |
| 3 | A review finds a count that mixed two things, and you add to your page of faults: 'the census paragraph mixed two counts'. |
| 4 | A review shows that a figure from your context pack is out of date, and you correct it in the instruction that quoted it. |
| 5 | Your handover note to a colleague retells the week and lists every figure so far. |
| 6 | The fresh chat checks the note's four claims, finds they hold, and starts on the next piece of work. |
| 7 | At the end of a long day you accept a piece of work because its review accepted it, and you could not say what it found. |

**Your question:** Which rule does each moment break?

**Answer key:**

1: one piece of work writes at a time (4.1). 2: nothing is deleted; withdrawn is a ruling, in a row of its own (4.1). 3: a line on the page names the fault as a rule, not the instance: 'define each figure before counting it' (4.2). 4: a correction goes where the fact lives, in the context pack, or the next instruction quotes the same wrong figure (4.2). 5: the note carries only what the files cannot, as claims; no figures and no story (4.3). 6: the fresh chat's first task is to check, then stop and wait (4.3). 7: cognitive debt: accept only what you can explain in your own words (this page).

### The concept

Keep a routine. Open the log, and answer where the work stands from it. Pick up the open piece of work with the earliest date: one writes at a time. Read the page of faults before each instruction. Add each new fault a review finds. Hand over before a chat gets long, and before a break.

Check the setup against what you actually did. After the first week, look at the log and the folders: a folder you never used, a page of faults you never opened, a review chat that kept memory on. Change the setup to fit the work.

Watch for cognitive debt, which grows with the number of pieces of work. Page 3.4 asked you to explain one accepted piece of work in your own words. With many under way, it is easy to accept the fifth because the first four were fine. Once a week, take one piece of work accepted that week and say, without the files, what it found and why the review believed it. If you cannot, read it again before anything is built on it.

And be clear about what the pattern does not claim. It does not make the output true: a review can miss what the instruction never asked. It does not make the work cheap: it costs a second chat, a log row and your time. It does not make two runs give the same answer. What it does is make every difference discussable and every failure countable. What was asked, what came back, how it was checked and who decided are all on file.

### In the tool

By hand, ten minutes at the start and end of a working day. At the start: run the status prompt of 4.1 on the log, and pick the one piece of work that writes today. During the day: read its kind's page of faults before you issue anything, and write each log row before you move on. At the end: check that every event of the day has its row, and write the handover note of 4.3 before a break of more than a few days.

Practical tips. Name each chat by piece of work, role and version, such as 'duplicates · review · v2', so that a list of chats reads like the log. Start the review in a new chat every time, version 2 included. Keep the folder somewhere it is copied, such as a synced OneDrive or SharePoint folder.

### AI usage tip — Write your readiness statement

**What the prompt does:** At the end of the by-hand route, it is easy to plan to use everything and then use nothing. A short statement of what you will do alone, what you will do with a review, and when you will look again, is a plan you can keep, with or without the plugin of Module 5.

**Prompt template (copy-paste into the assistant):**

```text
Below are my log from the pieces of work I ran in Modules 3 and 4 [paste it] and a list of the kinds of work I do in a normal month [list them, with how often]. Help me write a readiness statement in three parts. (1) What I now do alone, without the cycle, because none of the five conditions holds: more than three documents to read; a document that will last; a figure someone will quote; a change to a file; evidence the assistant cannot see for itself. (2) What I do with an instruction and a separate review, and which page of faults each uses. (3) What I will revisit, and when: a date, and what would make me change the statement. Base it on my log and my list only. Keep it under 200 words. Ask me about anything you cannot tell from them.
```

**Inputs and outputs:** Input: your log and the kinds of work you do. Output: a readiness statement of under 200 words, in three parts.

**Safeguard:** The statement is yours, not the chat's. Strike anything you would not actually do in a busy week; a shorter statement you keep is worth more than a complete one you do not.

**Time:** ~15 min · **Watch for:** A statement that promises the cycle for everything.

**Practise on Progressa — input:**

A head of the planning unit's month: four replies to schools' letters, one census count for the Permanent Secretary, two summaries of donor reports for the unit, one terms of reference for a consultant. Their log: one cycle, the census count, not accepted at version 1, accepted at version 2.

**Practise on Progressa — answer key:**

Alone: the four replies, and the donor summaries kept within the unit. With the cycle: the census count (checking) and the terms of reference (drafting: it lasts and goes outside). Revisit: after the next census, or when a donor summary is first quoted outside the unit. A good statement does not put the replies through the cycle.

**With the kit:** the orchestrator plugin (in preparation) does the bookkeeping of this routine: it starts each work chat and its review chat, writes the log, and says where the work stands when you ask. Module 5 builds a small prototype with it. The rules on this page stay yours: the parallel mistake of 4.1 happened with the plugin running. Optional: the cycle runs by hand.

## 4. Production notes

### 4.1 Design standard

A public servant with the page on one half of the screen and an assistant on the other must be able to do the page's step on their own work. By the end of 4.4 they have a log they answer from, a page of faults with a line of their own, a handover note checked by a fresh chat, and a readiness statement.

### 4.2 Voice and tone

As in Module 3. The record's codes, file names and the plugin's terms are never used on a page. The owner's rulings are described, not quoted.

### 4.3 The record behind the examples

Every Try it first is rewritten from the record of the registry prototype, frozen on 31 October 2026 in examples/plr/. The week's figures (23 closed, 13 at the first version, 10 second attempts, 4 withdrawn and run again) are those of the frozen record. The seven moments of 4.4 are written for the page, one for each rule this module takes from the record.

### 4.4 The plugin

The plugin appears only in the With the kit lines of 4.1, 4.3 and 4.4, which name no product. The setup of the computer is on page 3.2, where the learner first needs it.
