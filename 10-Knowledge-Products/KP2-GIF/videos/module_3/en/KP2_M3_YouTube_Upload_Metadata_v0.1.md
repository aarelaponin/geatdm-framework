### 3.1 — Why a bus needs an owner

**File:** `video/KP2_M3_3.1_Video_v0.4.mp4` · **Subtitles:** `audio/KP2_M3_3.1_Audio_v0.4.srt`

**Title:** Why interoperability platforms die in year two — and the owner that prevents it

**Description:**

```text
An interoperability platform with no owner decays in its second year — name the
owner before the first member joins, and split the body that sets the rules from
the body that runs the bus.

GIF · Module 3 · 3.1 — Why a bus needs an owner

Platforms rarely fail technically. The launch spotlight moves on, certificates
expire, nobody helps members adjust, nobody brings the next ministry in. The
cure is an Operating Authority at whole-of-government level — a standing body
with budget, staff and mandate, not a quarterly committee — starting with eight
to fifteen people and growing to thirty to eighty at national scale. Then the
trap: when one office both runs the bus and judges compliance, the waiver always
wins. Split the regulator from the operator — in a small administration, two
units inside one agency with separate mandates — and leave the member
ministries the third hat: their own services, data and compliance.

Progressa is a demonstration country, fictional on purpose.
```

### 3.2 — The three tiers of governance

**File:** `video/KP2_M3_3.2_Video_v0.2.mp4` · **Subtitles:** `audio/KP2_M3_3.2_Audio_v0.2.srt`

**Title:** The three tiers of interoperability governance — and what each one decides

**Description:**

```text
Strategic Council, Steering Committee, Technical Working Groups — political
authority, programme leadership and the specialists, each with one job.

GIF · Module 3 · 3.2 — The three tiers of governance

Governance fails in two opposite directions. Top-heavy: ministers asked to judge
technical detail, and decisions stall. Bottom-heavy: technicians make political
and financial commitments they have no authority to keep, and they get reversed
at budget review. The three tiers put each decision with the body equipped to
make it — the Council removes political obstacles, the Committee holds the
budget and prioritises the use-case catalogue, the Working Groups set the
standards — with the Operating Authority running the platform underneath. A
dispute escalates one level at a time, and only reaches ministers when it is
political.
```

### 3.3 — The RACI matrix

**File:** `video/KP2_M3_3.3_Video_v0.4.mp4` · **Subtitles:** `audio/KP2_M3_3.3_Audio_v0.4.srt`

**Title:** The one-page matrix that stops interoperability onboarding becoming a turf fight

**Description:**

```text
Write down who decides, who runs, who is consulted, once — it is what stops
every onboarding turning into a turf fight.

GIF · Module 3 · 3.3 — The RACI matrix

The barrier to interoperability is rarely technical; it is who decides.
Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed — and the iron rule: exactly one
Accountable per decision. Two means deadlock, zero means drift. Map only the
recurring decisions — admit a member, approve a standard, authorise an exchange,
resolve a dispute, change or retire a standard, suspend a member — plus two rows
that make the regulator/operator split concrete. One row worked through: admit a
new member. Agree the matrix before the first member joins, while it is still
uncontested.
```

### 3.4 — Member obligations

**File:** `video/KP2_M3_3.4_Video_v0.10.mp4` · **Subtitles:** `audio/KP2_M3_3.4_Audio_v0.10.srt`

**Title:** What an agency really signs up to when it joins an interoperability bus

**Description:**

```text
What an agency signs up to when it joins is the difference between a federation
and a free-for-all.

GIF · Module 3 · 3.4 — Member obligations

Connecting is not the job; what happens after the connection is. Eight common
obligations — stay connected, meet the service levels, keep data accurate,
answer the queries you owe and honour once-only, protect what you receive,
follow the framework's standards, name a Technical Focal Point and a Data
Protection Officer, and pay your share of the running cost. Provider and
consumer carry different weight: treat them the same and providers quietly
resist joining. A decree may compel connection, but a member agreement a CIO
signs turns the obligations into a commitment the Operating Authority can point
to.
```

### 3.5 — The four Technical Working Groups

**File:** `video/KP2_M3_3.5_Video_v0.6.mp4` · **Subtitles:** `audio/KP2_M3_3.5_Audio_v0.6.srt`

**Title:** The four standing groups that keep a growing interoperability bus coherent

**Description:**

```text
Security, semantics, APIs, platform operations — the four standing groups that
keep the bus coherent as it grows.

GIF · Module 3 · 3.5 — The four Technical Working Groups

Add twenty agencies with no one owning the technical work and you get hundreds
of incompatible ad hoc rules. Four groups, each owning one dimension: security
(trust model, access control), APIs (standards, service contracts, message
formats), platform operations (running the bus and connecting new members) and
semantics — the hardest, because agreeing that "enrolment" means the same to an
examination authority and a learner registry is a business decision, written
precisely enough for systems to use. And standing, not project: a project team
disbands at launch; a Working Group persists for the life of the framework,
deciding within its competence and recommending the rest up to the Steering
Committee.
```

### 3.6 — Governance as living configuration

**File:** `video/KP2_M3_3.6_Video_v0.6.mp4` · **Subtitles:** `audio/KP2_M3_3.6_Audio_v0.6.srt`

**Title:** Why interoperability governance must be living configuration, not a launch-day binder

**Description:**

```text
Change control and a named standards-portfolio owner keep the framework current
instead of frozen at launch.

GIF · Module 3 · 3.6 — Governance as living configuration

After launch the framework changes constantly — new standards, retired
protocols, a new security model — and without a controlled way to absorb them
the blueprint drifts from what actually runs. A named standards-portfolio owner
inside the Operating Authority keeps the register of current standards, versions
and exceptions; change control means one accountable body decides each change.
Two more standing assets: a conformance regime — self-assessment, third-party
check or operator test suite, a transition period for legacy systems,
re-certification every two years — and a semantic registry, stewarded by the
operator and owned by the sector data owners. Together with the tiers, the
RACI, the member agreements and the Working Group charters, they make up the
Governance Pack.
```
