## Compliance note — read before filming

This video is a **deliberate exception to rule 4.3 of the module bundles, "No individuals on screen".** The rule allows only an AI avatar or a screen-only voice-over. A named person on camera breaks it, intentionally and once, at the entrance to the course; every one of the thirty-seven videos after it stays without a person on screen, so the rule holds for all of the teaching content.

The video does **not** break the rule that each video stands alone with no introduction inside it: the introduction is a video of its own, and 1.1 to 5.10 remain self-contained and can each be found and watched alone.

**It belongs among the course's open calibration items**, beside the structural calls of section 5.3 of the Module 1 bundle. If the exception is declined, **the same words are recorded as a voice-over on a text-only title slide** (ITU template, background #E5F5FB, Arial), and nothing else in this script changes: no line depends on the face.

## Script

> _Frame: the presenter on camera, medium close-up, plain background, eyes to the lens. No slide. Two seconds of silence before the first word. Lower third at 0:02–0:07 — 'Aare Lapõnin · FiscalAdmin OÜ' (Arial 18pt, white on #003D5B, lower left)._

> _Position 1 — the citizen's errand. Say it as something you have watched happen, not as a complaint._

A parent enrolling a child is sent for a birth certificate another office holds. The offices cannot ask each other.

> _Position 2 — what should happen instead. The outcome comes before any reason. In the long version the rule's name follows its plain words._

Advanced administrations have settled this: the school obtains it, lawfully, with the parent's say, and asks once.⟦ The rule is called once-only.⟧

> _Beat. Position 3 — the four things, and one language. This is the turn of the video; slow down on the first sentence, then give the four evenly, the technology last._

Computers are the easy part. Policy and technical people build four things together, in one language: the law allowing it, an agreement on who gives what and who governs it, the same meaning of a word on both sides, and the technology.

> _Position 4 — no office is failing, in one sentence. Plainly, as a fact, not as a kindness._

No office is failing: one contract cannot deliver this; a framework planned once for the whole government can, and every tender enforces it.

> _Position 5 — nobody is far ahead, the pattern is public, the country stays in charge. The whole position is cut in the one-minute version._

⟦Nobody is far ahead: the most advanced administrations have made asking once a rule and a measure, and even they are only part of the way. The pattern is proven and public. GovStack, an international initiative, describes the part that carries the exchange, so your government can hold suppliers to it. Your country adapts it to its own law and stays in charge.⟧

> _Beat. Position 6 — who the course is for, and what each will be able to do. Name the roles one by one, a small pause between them._

The course serves whoever makes the case, drafts the law, runs or builds the exchange, or heads an office holding or using data. It runs from a signed mandate to a learner asked once.⟦ It starts where your government knows which systems it has; one that does not begins with the companion course on planning.⟧⟦ The work takes years, and a first service people can see comes long before the whole country is connected.⟧⟦ Each video ends with an exercise that turns your country's material, with an AI assistant, into a first draft of a document you need.⟧

> _Position 7 — where to begin. Two instructions, said slowly._

Making the case or drafting rules? Start with 1.1. Building? Start with 4.1.

> _Hold two seconds after the last word. Do not smile out. Cut on the still frame._

## Why the script reads as it does

**The order.** It follows the accepted message analysis, section 7.1: the errand, what should happen instead, the four things and one language, no office failing, nobody far ahead, who it is for and what each can do, where to begin. It does not follow the order of the reasoning behind the course (the experience of other countries, interoperability, the exchange component, the other layers, the course), nor the storyboard of the companion site. The presenter is named by the lower third, not by a spoken self-introduction, so that the first words a viewer hears are about a parent at a school.

**It does not open where the KP1 introduction opens.** KP1's introduction begins with a government's systems; this one begins with one person sent for a paper. KP1's turn is a plan for the whole government; this one turns on the statement that the computers are the easy part. KP1's argument that nobody is failing takes one sentence here, with the lever that belongs to this course: once the framework exists, every tender enforces it.

**The technology is never the headline.** It is the fourth of four things, named last, after the law, the agreement and the meaning. The component that carries the exchange is described in plain words in position 5 and not named.

**No office is blamed.** Position 4 says that one contract cannot deliver the exchange and that a framework planned once can. Nothing in the script says that an office hoards data, refuses to share or prefers paper.

**One name, said once, and only where it can be cut.** GovStack appears only in position 5, with its meaning in plain words. "Once-only" is said once, after its plain meaning, in the long version only; the one-minute version carries the meaning without the name. The authorship and address of the published material belong in the written description under the video.

**No promise the course cannot keep.** The script names no country, no figure of time or money, and no running system. The course is said to run "to a learner asked once", which is what the viewer learns to build; the recorded demonstration is not offered as something to watch running.

## Delivery notes

- **Register.** A senior adviser speaking to a colleague: collegial, unhurried, certain. Not a presenter and not a pitch. If it sounds like selling the course, it is one notch too bright.
- **Pace.** About 135 words a minute, slower than natural speech. The beats marked in the cues buy the time; take them rather than slowing every word.
- **Position 1 is the line that keeps the viewer.** Say it as something you have seen at a counter. The viewer must recognise the errand before anything is explained.
- **Position 3 is the turn.** Slow down at "Computers are the easy part." Then give the four things at an even pace, each with a small pause, and let "the technology" land last and lightly.
- **Position 4 is one sentence.** Do not break it into a list. "No office is failing" is said as a plain fact, not as a kindness.
- **The one name.** "GovStack" comes in position 5 only, followed at once by what it is; if that position is cut, it is not heard. Say it the way the course's narrated videos say it, so the series sounds as one.
- **"1.1" and "4.1".** Say "one point one" and "four point one", not "video one dot one". They are the last thing heard, and they are instructions.
- **Direct address.** "Your government", "your country" — never "one might". If a line drifts away from the viewer, it is the wrong line.
- **Not on camera.** No organisation that commissioned the course, no contract number, no country, no named AI product ("an AI assistant"), no figure promising speed or saving. Attribution belongs in the description and on the end card, where it can be corrected without filming again.
