## "What an EA actually is"

**This document is the sole authority for the audio.** Everything the hosts say must come from
this file. Do not add examples, statistics, institutions, countries, analogies, or closing
questions that do not appear below.

## 1. Framing rule — the single most important instruction

**The listener is the government official, not the citizen being served.**

Address the listener as the person who *runs* these systems and can *fix* them. Use
"in your ministry", "your programme", "your vendor", "your minister".

- ❌ Do **not** open with, or return to, the listener standing at a counter filling in a form,
  waiting in a queue, or being frustrated by a service.
- ❌ Do **not** use "we've all been there" / consumer-complaint framing.
- ✅ The citizen appears only as the person **your** systems burden — third person: "the citizen
  who gave her ID number to the health ministry gives it again, on paper, at the school."

## 2. Slide-by-slide time budget and content

Cover the slides **in order**. Finish each slide's content before moving to the next. End each
slide's segment on a complete sentence — do not let a thought straddle a slide boundary.

**Word budget: about 560 spoken words in total** (140 words per minute × the runtime in §0).
The v0.1 take ran ~842 words and the v0.2 take ~1,173 — both far over. The overrun is words,
not only pace. Cut words per segment; do not speak faster.

**No metaphor is permitted in this video.** "The agreed picture" is the deck's own term, used
throughout — it is content, not a metaphor to extend.

### Slide 1 — Title card · 0:00–0:15 (15 s)

Cold open, no music bed, no "welcome to the show", **no hypothetical scenario, no price tag** —
the v0.1 take opened with "welcome to today's Deep Dive" and the v0.2 take with an invented
hundred-million-dollar portal story. Both openings are automatic rejects. Host A states the topic in one or two
sentences, in this spirit:

> "Module 1, video 1.2 — what an Enterprise Architecture actually is. An EA is the picture
> everyone agrees describes your government — minister, ministry CIO, donor, vendor."

Host B adds one sentence naming the stake: with that picture, you lead the conversation about
what comes next; without it, others lead it for you. Then move on. **No preamble about the
sources.**

### Slide 2 — What an EA is made of · 0:15–0:45 (30 s)

Host B, plainly:

- An Enterprise Architecture is a set of documents and diagrams. Together, they describe how
  your government works.
- What services it delivers, and to whom. What data it holds, and who owns it. What software
  supports those services. What infrastructure runs underneath.

No questions from Host A in this segment.

### Slide 3 — The agreed picture — not software you buy · 0:45–1:25 (40 s)

- An EA **is**: the agreed picture of your government; a set of documents and diagrams; the same
  description everyone uses — minister, CIO, donor, vendor.
- An EA **is not**: software. Not a vendor product. Not a tool you procure.
- **Host B states the defensive point directly:** when a vendor says "our product gives you an
  EA", the answer is no — it does not. This slide is your first line of defence against that
  pitch.

Host A may ask **one** short clarifying question here. Not more.

### Slide 4 — Minister, donor, vendor — all use the same picture · 1:25–2:10 (45 s)

Name all four users of the picture, in this order, briskly:

1. **Your minister** — to brief cabinet. They cannot describe what the country's digital spend
   is buying without an agreed picture of what the systems are.
2. **The donor** — before funding the next programme. They want to see how their investment fits
   with the others.
3. **The vendor** — to match what they propose to what is already there.
4. **You** — to keep all three aligned: the donor funds what the country needs, the vendor
   builds what fits, and the minister tells a coherent story.

### Slide 5 — The picture has four parts · 2:10–3:05 (55 s)

**Number the parts out loud — "part one" … "part four" — roughly 13 seconds each, in this
order.** The order is deliberate: data comes before applications.

1. **Services** — what your government does, and for whom. The layer your minister talks about;
   the layer the public sees.
2. **Data** — the information your government holds, who owns it, where the authoritative copy
   lives.
3. **Applications** — the software that uses the data to deliver the services. What gets bought,
   built, integrated, replaced.
4. **Technology** — the infrastructure underneath: networks, hosting, identity, security.

**Host B lands this line and lets it sit a beat:** applications come and go; technology cycles
every decade; **data outlasts them all.** That is why data comes before applications in the
picture.

### Slide 6 — Written down, a list of projects becomes a system · 3:05–3:30 (25 s)

- When you write down all four parts, your government's digital landscape stops being a list of
  unrelated projects. It starts looking like a system.
- A system you can change deliberately. A system you can plan investments against. A system you
  can talk about across ministries, with donors and vendors, without re-explaining the basics
  every time.

### Slide 7 — In one sentence · 3:30–3:50 (20 s)

Host B delivers the one-sentence close, unhurried:

- That is what an Enterprise Architecture is. Not software. Not a tool. The agreed picture of
  your government — and the tool that lets you, instead of the vendor or the donor, lead the
  conversation about what comes next.

**Close on the series handoff, spoken by Host B, as a statement — not a question:** the next
video looks at why the projects your government already runs cannot produce this picture by
themselves. The v0.2 take phrased this handoff as a reflective question to the listener and
dropped the sources line — do neither.

### Slide 8 — Sources · 3:50–4:00 (10 s)

One line only, then stop: "Sources are in the video description." **Do not read URLs. Do not
name section numbers. Do not summarise the sources.** These are the final words of the
audio. Nothing follows them, and no question precedes them — the take ends here.

## 3. Hard prohibitions

Every category below appeared in the previous take. None may recur.

- ❌ **No podcast outro.** No "that raises a fascinating question for you to consider", no "look
  around at the other institutions in your life", no invitation to reflect on other sectors. The
  audio ends on the series handoff in §2, Slide 7, plus the one-line sources note.
- ❌ **No "deep dive" self-reference.** Never say "deep dive", "today we're unpacking", "welcome
  to", "our sources", "the sources say", "here's where it gets interesting".
- ❌ **No invented specifics.** No named country, no cost figures or dollar amounts, no
  percentages, no dates, no institutions other than those in §2, and no invented opening
  scenario — the v0.2 take fabricated a hundred-million-dollar portal launch.
- ❌ **No filler.** Remove "you know", "like", "I mean", "basically", "totally", "sort of",
  "right?", "wow", "oh absolutely", "man". Reaction interjections should be rare and short.
- ❌ **No backchannel.** While one host speaks, the other stays silent. No single-word
  confirmations — "Right", "Exactly", "Mm-hmm", "Okay", "Sure", "Wow", "Got it". The previous
  take spent a large share of its overrun on these. Each host speaks in complete turns of full
  sentences.
- ❌ **No consumer-outrage vocabulary.** Not "broken", "chaos", "held hostage", "extortionate",
  "nightmare". Say: fragmented, duplicated, costly to change, locked in.
- ❌ **No idioms or metaphors.** None is permitted in this video (§2). The previous takes used
  a map-and-city analogy, a bricks-and-blueprint analogy, "organizational superpower" and
  "master plan" — all rejected. "The agreed picture" is the only image, and it is the deck's
  term, not a metaphor to extend. Much of this audience
  listens in English as an additional language and the audio is subtitled and translated.
- ❌ **No crosstalk or interruption.** One speaker finishes, the other begins. The audio is cut
  to slide transitions, so overlapping speech breaks the edit.
- ❌ **No in-video intro or outro branding.** ITU compliance rule — the video opens on content
  and closes on the sources line.

## 5. Definition of done

The audio is correct when:

1. Total runtime is 3:50–4:10.
2. A listener can tell, without seeing the slides, where each of the eight slides begins.
3. The four parts are numbered aloud, in the deck's order — Services, Data, Applications,
   Technology — with data before applications.
4. Nothing is said that is not in this brief.
5. The final words are the series handoff plus the one-line sources note.
6. There are audible pauses at the slide boundaries, long enough to cut against.
