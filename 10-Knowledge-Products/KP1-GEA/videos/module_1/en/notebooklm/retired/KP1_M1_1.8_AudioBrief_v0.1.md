## 1. Framing rule — the single most important instruction

**The listener is the government official, not the citizen being served.**

Address the listener as the person who *runs* these systems and can *fix* them. Use
"your country", "your minister", "your programme".

- ❌ Do **not** open with, or return to, the listener standing at a counter filling in a form,
  waiting in a queue, or being frustrated by a service.
- ❌ Do **not** use "we've all been there" / consumer-complaint framing.
- ✅ The citizen appears only inside the country descriptions, third person, exactly as §2 gives
  them.

## 2. Slide-by-slide time budget and content

Cover the slides **in order**. Finish each slide's content before moving to the next. End each
slide's segment on a complete sentence — do not let a thought straddle a slide boundary.

**One permitted expression: "polestar"** — the deck's word for Estonia. Use it at most twice,
only about Estonia, and always paired with "not a template to copy". No other idiom or metaphor
is permitted.

**The only countries named in this video are Rwanda, Kenya, South Africa and Estonia.** Every
fact about them comes from this brief — no additions, no other countries, no comparisons to
countries not named here.

**Tone rule for Kenya:** honesty about the contested experience is a feature, not a defect.
Mixed results are stated factually — no outrage vocabulary, no mockery, no verdict on whether
Kenya "failed".

### Slide 1 — Title card · 0:00–0:15 (15 s)

Cold open, no music bed, no "welcome to the show". Host A states the topic in one or two
sentences, in this spirit:

> "Module 1, video 1.8 — four signposts. Three African, one international. Four countries did
> the work — and the pattern shows in all of them."

Host B adds one sentence naming the stake: the pattern travels, and your country can apply it
too. Then move on. **No preamble about the sources.**

### Slide 2 — Four countries did the work · 0:15–0:40 (25 s)

- Four countries did the work. Three of them are in Africa. They differ in size, in resources,
  in governance type — and the EA pattern shows in all three.
- The fourth is Estonia — the most-cited international example, useful as a reference but with a
  very different starting context.
- Looking at the four together shows what the pattern looks like in practice.

### Slide 3 — Rwanda — small country, strong centre · 0:40–1:15 (35 s)

- Rwanda. The Ministry of ICT and Innovation, with **Irembo** as the unifying citizen-services
  platform.
- Starting from a small base in the mid-2010s, Rwanda built one of the most ambitious
  digital-government programmes on the continent: a single citizen-service platform, a national
  ID linked across services, strong central coordination.
- The institutional muscle is small but disciplined.
- **The lesson:** in a smaller country with political will, the lifecycle can be compressed —
  and the gains in the second and third sectors come faster than the first.

### Slide 4 — Kenya — the obstacles are concrete and documented · 1:15–1:55 (40 s)

- Kenya. The **Huduma Centres** — physical one-stop shops where citizens access many government
  services in one place — with the **Huduma Namba** programme attempting to provide the
  unifying digital identity underneath.
- The results are mixed, and openly debated in Kenya's public arena. Obstacles were met in
  courts, in parliament, and in implementation.
- **The lesson, stated carefully by Host B:** the lesson is not "Kenya solved this." It is that
  Kenya tried, the obstacles are documented — legal, political, technical — and the debate tells
  you what to plan for.

Host A may ask **one** short clarifying question here. Not more.

### Slide 5 — South Africa — where you cannot impose, you federate · 1:55–2:35 (40 s)

- South Africa. The State Information Technology Agency — **SITA** — and the federated
  digital-government model.
- South Africa has strong provincial governments and constitutionally autonomous statutory
  bodies. A single architecture cannot be imposed from the top.
- SITA acts as a coordinating body: shared standards, common procurement frameworks, a
  maintained reference architecture that agencies **adopt rather than have imposed**.
- **The lesson:** wherever sub-national governments or autonomous bodies carry real authority,
  the federated model — coordination without coercion — is the realistic pattern. Many countries
  with strong provincial or county governments will recognise this shape.

### Slide 6 — Estonia — the polestar, not the template · 2:35–3:20 (45 s)

- Estonia. The Information System Authority — **RIA** — and the most-cited international example
  of mature digital government.
- Starting in the late 1990s, Estonia built **X-Road** as the data-exchange backbone;
  distributed state registers — population, business, land — each owned by its accountable
  agency; and the **once-only principle**: the state never asks a citizen for the same
  information twice.
- Today, almost every public service in Estonia runs online.
- **Host B states the caution plainly:** Estonia is a small unitary state with very different
  starting conditions. Use it as a polestar — what fully mature digital government can look
  like — not as a template to copy directly. The pattern is the same. The path is your
  country's.

### Slide 7 — Four very different countries — the same four elements · 3:20–3:50 (30 s)

**Number the four elements out loud — "one" … "four":**

1. A small central team with real authority.
2. A published framework that other agencies adopt rather than fight.
3. A governance mechanism that is binding, not advisory.
4. A time horizon measured in years for full maturity — with intermediate results visible
   inside months.

These are not outliers. This is what committing to the lifecycle, the team, the governance and
the time horizon looks like in practice.

### Slide 8 — In one sentence · 3:50–4:20 (30 s)

Host B delivers the close, unhurried:

- **The pattern travels.** Across small countries and large. Across unitary states and
  federations. Across countries with strong central authority and countries where authority is
  distributed.
- Your country can apply it too — once your minister commits to the four asks from the previous
  video.

**Close on the series handoff, spoken by Host B:** the rest of this knowledge product shows you
how to do the work — using a fictional country called **Progressa**, so every step is visible in
detail.

### Slide 9 — Sources · 4:20–4:30 (10 s)

One line only, then stop: "Sources are in the video description." **Do not read URLs. Do not
name section numbers. Do not summarise the sources.**

## 3. Hard prohibitions

- ❌ **No podcast outro.** No "that raises a fascinating question for you to consider", no "look
  around at the other institutions in your life", no invitation to reflect on other sectors. The
  audio ends on the series handoff in §2, Slide 8, plus the one-line sources note.
- ❌ **No "deep dive" self-reference.** Never say "deep dive", "today we're unpacking", "welcome
  to", "our sources", "the sources say", "here's where it gets interesting".
- ❌ **No invented specifics.** No countries beyond the four in §2, no cost figures, no
  percentages, no population or adoption statistics, no institutions beyond those in §2. The
  only dates permitted are "the mid-2010s" (Rwanda) and "the late 1990s" (Estonia).
- ❌ **No verdicts on Kenya.** Not "failure", not "disaster", not "cautionary tale". The brief's
  framing — tried, contested, documented, useful — is the whole verdict.
- ❌ **No filler.** Remove "you know", "like", "I mean", "basically", "totally", "sort of",
  "right?", "wow", "oh absolutely", "man". Reaction interjections should be rare and short.
- ❌ **No consumer-outrage vocabulary.** Not "broken", "chaos", "held hostage", "extortionate",
  "nightmare". Say: fragmented, duplicated, costly to change, locked in.
- ❌ **No idioms or metaphors beyond "polestar" as permitted in §2.** Much of this audience
  listens in English as an additional language and the audio is subtitled and translated.
- ❌ **No crosstalk or interruption.** One speaker finishes, the other begins. The audio is cut
  to slide transitions, so overlapping speech breaks the edit.
- ❌ **No in-video intro or outro branding.** ITU compliance rule — the video opens on content
  and closes on the sources line.

## 5. Definition of done

The audio is correct when:

1. Total runtime is 4:20–4:40.
2. A listener can tell, without seeing the slides, where each of the nine slides begins.
3. The four countries appear in the deck's order — Rwanda, Kenya, South Africa, Estonia — each
   closing on its stated lesson.
4. Kenya is handled factually: tried, contested, documented — no verdict language.
5. The four common elements on Slide 7 are numbered aloud.
6. Nothing is said that is not in this brief.
7. The final words are the series handoff — including Progressa — plus the one-line sources
   note.
8. There are audible pauses at the slide boundaries, long enough to cut against.
