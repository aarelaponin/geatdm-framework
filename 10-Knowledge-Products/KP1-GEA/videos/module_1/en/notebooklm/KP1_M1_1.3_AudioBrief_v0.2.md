## 1. Framing rule — the single most important instruction

**The listener is the government official, not the citizen being served.**

Address the listener as the person who *runs* these systems and can *fix* them. Use
"in your ministry", "your programme", "your vendor", "your minister".

- ❌ Do **not** open with, or return to, the listener standing at a counter filling in a form,
  waiting in a queue, or being frustrated by a service.
- ❌ Do **not** use "we've all been there" / consumer-complaint framing.
- ✅ The citizen appears only as the person **your** systems burden — third person: "the citizen
  still fills the same form five times."

## 2. Slide-by-slide time budget and content

Cover the slides **in order**. Finish each slide's content before moving to the next. End each
slide's segment on a complete sentence — do not let a thought straddle a slide boundary.

**Word budget: about 630 spoken words in total** (140 words per minute × the runtime in §0).
The previous take ran ~1041 words at ~175 wpm — the overrun was words, not only pace. Cut
words per segment; do not speak faster.

**No metaphor is permitted in this video.** "The math changes" is the deck's own phrase for the
cost comparison — keep it literal, do not build imagery around it.

**Tone rule for this video: the math, not the morality.** No blame lands on project teams at any
point. They are behaving rationally.

### Slide 1 — Title card · 0:00–0:15 (15 s)

Cold open, no music bed, no "welcome to the show". Host A states the topic in one or two
sentences, in this spirit:

> "Module 1, video 1.3 — why projects can't do this themselves. Procurement rules can require
> interoperability. They cannot deliver it."

Host B adds one sentence naming the stake: only planning at the level of the whole government,
supported by reference architectures, can. Then move on. **No preamble about the sources.**

### Slide 2 — Your country already requires this · 0:15–0:50 (35 s)

Host A voices the objection the listener is already thinking, then Host B answers:

- You may be thinking: my country already requires this. Every new digital project must specify
  open APIs. Every new contract must require interoperability. The national digital strategy is
  signed by cabinet.
- So why does the citizen still fill the same form five times? Why do new programmes still build
  their own version of identity and payments?
- **Host B states the uncomfortable answer plainly:** procurement rules can require behaviour.
  They cannot make that behaviour the cheapest choice for the project doing the work.

### Slide 3 — Each project is rational — and builds its own · 0:50–1:30 (40 s)

- Inside any new programme, the team is rational. They have a contract, a budget, a deadline.
- The question every project asks: "How do we ship on time and on budget?"
- Re-using another ministry's identity system means learning that system, negotiating with that
  ministry's team, accepting their delays. Building your own version is faster and simpler.
- So the team builds its own. **That is not a failure of discipline. That is the project doing
  exactly what it was funded to do.**

Host A may ask **one** short clarifying question here. Not more.

### Slide 4 — The math changes only at whole-of-government level · 1:30–2:20 (50 s)

**This is the most important slide. Give it room; do not rush it.**

- Re-use becomes rational only when you can see across the whole government. From that view, the
  math changes.
- Walk the four ministries in order: the first ministry pays to build the identity system. The
  second does not — it consumes the first. The third does not. The fourth does not.
- Over five years, the country saves a meaningful share of its sectoral digital spend. Say it
  exactly that way — **no percentage, no figure.**
- **Host B lands the insight and lets it sit a beat:** this view does not exist inside any
  single project. It exists only at the level of your country's whole digital portfolio. That is
  the view an EA gives you — and the view your minister needs to make funding decisions.

### Slide 5 — Two more things projects cannot deliver · 2:20–3:00 (40 s)

**Number them out loud — "the first" and "the second" — roughly 20 seconds each.**

1. **Sustainability.** A project ships on time and moves on. Twelve years from now, the original
   vendor is gone, the open-source library has forked, the technology has moved on — and the
   system is still in service, with nobody who fully understands it. Projects are not
   incentivised to plan for that moment. They are incentivised to ship.
2. **Complexity reduction.** A project says yes to most feature requests, because requests come
   from people the project must please. Five years later, the system is too complex to maintain
   or change. Saying no requires authority the project does not have.

### Slide 6 — A reference architecture is other countries' learning, written down · 3:00–3:40 (40 s)

- Reference architectures answer the questions projects do not.
- Other countries have built these systems for decades. They learned what survives technology
  change and what does not — which architectural decisions still hold up after twenty years, and
  which cost them dearly.
- A reference architecture is that learning, written down. **PAERA is one such reference.**
- Adopting it means starting with their lessons, instead of paying for the same lessons yourself
  over the next twenty years.

### Slide 7 — In one sentence · 3:40–4:20 (40 s)

Host B delivers the close, unhurried:

- When you make the case for an EA, the case is this: the four-part picture is the artefact.
  **Planning is the function.**
- Re-use, sustainability, complexity reduction — three things every digital ministry says it
  wants — none of them come from projects alone. All of them come from the planning view an EA
  gives, and from the reference architectures that EA practice connects you to.
- That is why projects cannot fix this themselves. That is why an EA is work your minister must
  commission — separately, deliberately, with sustained funding.

**Close on the series handoff, spoken by Host B:** and if this planning case feels familiar,
the next video adds the newer half of the argument — why an EA matters more now than it did ten
years ago.

### Slide 8 — Sources · 4:20–4:30 (10 s)

One line only, then stop: "Sources are in the video description." **Do not read URLs. Do not
name section numbers. Do not summarise the sources.** These are the final words of the
audio. Nothing follows them, and no question precedes them — the take ends here.

## 3. Hard prohibitions

Every category below appeared in the previous take. None may recur.

- ❌ **No podcast outro.** No "that raises a fascinating question for you to consider", no "look
  around at the other institutions in your life", no invitation to reflect on other sectors. The
  audio ends on the series handoff in §2, Slide 7, plus the one-line sources note.
- ❌ **No "deep dive" self-reference.** Never say "deep dive", "today we're unpacking", "welcome
  to", "our sources", "the sources say", "here's where it gets interesting".
- ❌ **No invented specifics.** No named country, no cost figures, no percentages, no dates,
  no institutions other than those in §2. "A meaningful share of its sectoral digital spend" is
  the only quantity permitted for the savings — never a number.
- ❌ **No blame on project teams.** Not "lazy", not "short-sighted", not "siloed thinking". The
  brief's framing is that teams act rationally within their funding.
- ❌ **No filler.** Remove "you know", "like", "I mean", "basically", "totally", "sort of",
  "right?", "wow", "oh absolutely", "man". Reaction interjections should be rare and short.
- ❌ **No backchannel.** While one host speaks, the other stays silent. No single-word
  confirmations — "Right", "Exactly", "Mm-hmm", "Okay", "Sure", "Wow", "Got it". The previous
  take spent a large share of its overrun on these. Each host speaks in complete turns of full
  sentences.
- ❌ **No consumer-outrage vocabulary.** Not "broken", "chaos", "held hostage", "extortionate",
  "nightmare". Say: fragmented, duplicated, costly to change, locked in.
- ❌ **No idioms or metaphors.** None is permitted in this video (§2). Much of this audience
  listens in English as an additional language and the audio is subtitled and translated.
- ❌ **No crosstalk or interruption.** One speaker finishes, the other begins. The audio is cut
  to slide transitions, so overlapping speech breaks the edit.
- ❌ **No in-video intro or outro branding.** ITU compliance rule — the video opens on content
  and closes on the sources line.
