## 1. Framing rule — the single most important instruction

**The listener is the government official, not the citizen being served.**

Address the listener as the person who *runs* these systems and can *fix* them. Use
"in your ministry", "your programme", "your vendor", "your minister".

- ❌ Do **not** open with, or return to, the listener standing at a counter filling in a form,
  waiting in a queue, or being frustrated by a service.
- ❌ Do **not** use "we've all been there" / consumer-complaint framing.
- ✅ The citizen appears only as the person **your** systems serve or burden — third person:
  "a citizen proves who they are at any service, without paper."

## 2. Slide-by-slide time budget and content

Cover the slides **in order**. Finish each slide's content before moving to the next. End each
slide's segment on a complete sentence — do not let a thought straddle a slide boundary.

**Word budget: about 630 spoken words in total** (140 words per minute × the runtime in §0).
The previous take ran ~877 words at ~175 wpm — the overrun was words, not only pace. Cut
words per segment; do not speak faster.

**No metaphor is permitted in this video.** "Shared language" and "in the same room, using the
same words" are the deck's own terms — content, not imagery to extend.

### Slide 1 — Title card · 0:00–0:15 (15 s)

Cold open, no music bed, no "welcome to the show", and no imagery — the previous take opened
on a "coat of paint on a crumbling house" metaphor; state the era shift plainly. Host A states
the topic in one or two sentences, in this spirit:

> "Module 1, video 1.4 — why an Enterprise Architecture matters more now. For thirty years,
> digital work meant putting paper online. That era is ending."

Host B adds one sentence naming the stake: the work now is to redesign how your ministry serves
citizens — and that work needs business and IT in the same room, using the same words. Then move
on. **No preamble about the sources.**

### Slide 2 — What digital work used to mean · 0:15–0:45 (30 s)

Host B, plainly:

- For a long time, digital transformation in government meant one thing: take a paper process
  and put it online.
- The application form becomes a web form. The queue becomes an online appointment. The
  certificate becomes a PDF.
- The ministry still does the same work, in the same order, with the same roles. Only the medium
  changes.

### Slide 3 — Putting paper online is no longer the main work · 0:45–1:10 (25 s)

- That work is still important. But it is no longer the most important work your ministry is
  being asked to do.
- The countries — and the ministries — delivering real citizen results today are not just
  digitising forms. They are redesigning how citizens are served.
- The contrast, stated once: in the old work, the operating model stays the same and business
  decides what while IT delivers how. In the new work, the operating model changes — and
  business and IT decide together.

### Slide 4 — The new work redesigns how citizens are served · 1:10–1:50 (40 s)

**Number the three examples out loud — "one" … "three" — roughly 12 seconds each, in this
order.** These three, and no others.

1. **One farmer register** — shared by the agriculture ministry, the cooperative bank, the input
   subsidy programme and the climate-resilience programme. The same farmer, recognised the same
   way, by all of them.
2. **One national identity** — a citizen proves who they are at any service, without paper.
3. **One learner record** — it follows the child from primary school to university.

**Host B closes the segment:** none of these is "put it online". Each is a redesign of how the
ministry works.

### Slide 5 — The new work needs business and IT to decide together · 1:50–2:25 (35 s)

- When you redesign how your ministry works, two groups must decide together.
- The business side: your minister, your director-general, your head of policy. The IT side:
  your architects, your engineers.
- In the old work, they did not need to talk much — business decided what; IT delivered how. In
  the new work, they decide together. About the same questions. With the same seriousness.

Host A may ask **one** short clarifying question here. Not more.

### Slide 6 — They sit in the same meeting — and miss each other · 2:25–3:05 (40 s)

**This is the recognition moment — the listener has lived this meeting. Give it room; do not
rush it.**

- But the two sides do not share a language.
- Your minister talks about citizen services and policy goals. Your chief architect talks about
  systems, APIs and data.
- They sit in the same meeting and miss each other.
- **Host B lands this and lets it sit a beat:** the decision does not get made well — or it does
  not get made at all. And the ministry stays in the old work, even though the new work is what
  is needed.

### Slide 7 — An EA gives both sides one language · 3:05–3:45 (40 s)

This is the second job an Enterprise Architecture does. **Number the three elements out loud —
"first" … "third" — in this order:**

1. **The agreed picture** — something both sides can point at.
2. **Plain words for the basic terms** — service, capability, data domain — meaning the same
   thing to both sides.
3. **A regular forum — the EA Board** — where business and IT sit together and decide.

With these in place, the redesign conversation finally happens. Without them, it does not.

### Slide 8 — In one sentence · 3:45–4:20 (35 s)

Host B delivers the close, unhurried:

- When you make the case to your minister, this is the second half of the case. Planning is one
  half. Shared language is the other.
- **In the era of digitising paper, an EA was useful. In the era of redesigning how the ministry
  works, an EA is necessary** — because without it, the business side and the IT side cannot
  have the conversation the redesign requires.

**Close on the series handoff, spoken by Host B:** so the case for an EA is made. The next
question is practical — which framework do you anchor it on? That is the next video, and the
answer is PAERA.

### Slide 9 — Sources · 4:20–4:30 (10 s)

One line only, then stop: "Sources are in the video description." **Do not read URLs. Do not
name section numbers. Do not summarise the sources.** These are the final words of the
audio. Nothing follows them, and no question precedes them — the take ends here.

## 3. Hard prohibitions

Every category below appeared in the previous take. None may recur.

- ❌ **No podcast outro.** No "that raises a fascinating question for you to consider", no "look
  around at the other institutions in your life", no invitation to reflect on other sectors. The
  audio ends on the series handoff in §2, Slide 8, plus the one-line sources note.
- ❌ **No "deep dive" self-reference.** Never say "deep dive", "today we're unpacking", "welcome
  to", "our sources", "the sources say",
  "according to the sources", "here's where it gets interesting".
- ❌ **No invented specifics.** No named country, no cost figures, no percentages, no dates,
  no institutions other than those in §2. "For thirty years" is the only era duration permitted.
- ❌ **No filler.** Remove "you know", "like", "I mean", "basically", "totally", "sort of",
  "right?", "wow", "oh absolutely", "man". Reaction interjections should be rare and short.
- ❌ **No backchannel.** While one host speaks, the other stays silent. No single-word
  confirmations — "Right", "Exactly", "Mm-hmm", "Okay", "Sure", "Wow", "Got it". The previous
  take spent a large share of its overrun on these. Each host speaks in complete turns of full
  sentences.
- ❌ **No consumer-outrage vocabulary.** Not "broken", "chaos", "held hostage", "extortionate",
  "nightmare". Say: fragmented, duplicated, costly to change, locked in.
- ❌ **No idioms or metaphors.** None is permitted in this video (§2). Much of this audience
  listens in English as an additional language and the audio is subtitled and translated.
- ❌ **No crosstalk or interruption.** One speaker finishes, the other begins. The audio is cut
  to slide transitions, so overlapping speech breaks the edit.
- ❌ **No in-video intro or outro branding.** ITU compliance rule — the video opens on content
  and closes on the sources line.
