## 1. Framing rule — the single most important instruction

**The listener is the government official, not the citizen being served.**

Address the listener as the person who *runs* these systems and can *fix* them. Use
"in your ministry", "your programme", "your vendor", "your minister".

- ❌ Do **not** open with, or return to, the listener standing at a counter filling in a form,
  waiting in a queue, or being frustrated by a service.
- ❌ Do **not** use "we've all been there" / consumer-complaint framing.
- ✅ The citizen does not need to appear in this video at all.

---

## 2. Slide-by-slide time budget and content

Cover the slides **in order**. Finish each slide's content before moving to the next. End each
slide's segment on a complete sentence — do not let a thought straddle a slide boundary.

**No metaphor is permitted in this video.** "The picture on the wall" is the deck's literal
advice about a printed page, not imagery to extend.

**Rhythm rule:** slides 4 to 8 are five phase cards with the same shape — question, deliverable,
sign-off, duration. Keep the same spoken pattern for each phase so the listener hears the
structure repeat.

**Ordering rule — this take is cut against the slides, so order is not a style choice.**
Cover slides 1 to 12 in strict numerical order and finish each before starting the next. Never
preview a later slide's material while an earlier slide is being covered.

**The deck was reordered in v0.3 to match how this content actually reads.** The minister's four
sign-offs all fall at the end of phases 1 to 4 — Discover, Assess, Adapt, Plan — so that summary
now sits at **slide 8, immediately after Phase 4 and before Phase 5**. Two earlier takes narrated
it there regardless of what the brief said; the deck now agrees with them. Phase 5 is slide 9 and
is the *last* phase discussed, closing on ongoing governance.

### Slide 1 — Title card · 0:00–0:15 (15 s)

Cold open, no music bed, no "welcome to the show". Host A states the topic in one or two
sentences, in this spirit:

> "Module 1, video 1.6 — the lifecycle on one page. Six months from start to a roadmap your
> minister can take to cabinet. Then ongoing governance."

Host B adds one sentence naming the shape: five phases, four sign-offs, one continuous practice.
Then move on. **No preamble about the sources.**

### Slide 2 — The one picture to take away · 0:15–0:40 (25 s)

- If you take one picture away from this knowledge product, take this one: the EA lifecycle on a
  single page.
- The picture that goes on the wall of the EA Board room. The picture your minister puts in
  every cabinet briefing. The picture your team points at when they explain where the work is.

**Retrieval moment — Host A asks the listener directly, then leaves a one-beat pause:**

> "Before we walk it — make a guess. From a standing start, how long until a roadmap your
> minister can take to cabinet? Hold that number."

Do not answer the question here. The answer arrives on Slide 11.

### Slide 3 — Five phases. Four sign-offs. Six months to a roadmap. · 0:40–1:00 (20 s)

- Five phases. Each one answers a single question. Each produces a single deliverable. Each ends
  with a sign-off by the senior decision-maker.
- The first four phases together take about six months. The fifth phase is ongoing — your
  country's permanent way of working.

### Slide 4 — Phase 1 — Discover · 1:00–1:30 (30 s)

**Say "Phase one. Discover." then follow the pattern:**

- The question: what exists today?
- Your architects map the current digital landscape — the strategies in force, the systems that
  exist, the sector plans, the stakeholders, the legal framework.
- The deliverable: a Discovery brief — the picture of where your country is, with no
  recommendations yet.
- The sign-off: the senior decision-maker confirms the picture is accurate enough to build on.
- Three to four weeks.

### Slide 5 — Phase 2 — Assess · 1:30–1:50 (20 s)

**"Phase two. Assess."**

- The question: what is the gap?
- Architects compare what Discovery found against PAERA-anchored standards — the current-state
  picture in the four parts of an EA, maturity scorecards, the gap analysis.
- The sign-off: the gap analysis reflects ground truth. Six to eight weeks.

### Slide 6 — Phase 3 — Adapt · 1:50–2:20 (30 s)

**"Phase three. Adapt."**

- The question: what fits our country? PAERA is a starting point, not a constraint.
- Architects work with sector CIOs and your EA Board to shape the framework to your country.
- For each building block, the question is: do we build it, do we buy it from the marketplace,
  do we share another country's, or do we test in a sandbox first?
- The sign-off: the framework and the build / buy / share approach. Four to six weeks.

### Slide 7 — Phase 4 — Plan · 2:20–2:40 (20 s)

**"Phase four. Plan."**

- The question: how do we get there?
- Architects describe the target state and sequence the work into a roadmap with investment
  estimates.
- The sign-off: roadmap approved, budget committed — by the senior decision-maker and the EA
  Board. This is the deliverable your minister can take to cabinet. Six to eight weeks.

### Slide 8 — Your minister reviews four times — not every diagram · 2:40–3:05 (25 s)

- Notice the rhythm: four sign-offs in six months. Your minister does not review every diagram.
  They review at four moments, each tied to a defined deliverable.
- Between sign-offs, the architects work.
- **Host B lands this line:** the minister's job in between is to remove obstacles — the
  political ones, mostly. The technical ones are why the architects were hired.

### Slide 9 — Phase 5 — Execute & Govern · 3:05–3:30 (25 s)

**"Phase five. Execute and Govern."**

- The question: how do we sustain this?
- The approved roadmap becomes a project pipeline. The small permanent EA team — two to four
  senior architects — turns the EA from a one-time delivery into a living repository.
- The EA Board reviews new projects against the architecture, approves cross-ministry decisions,
  enforces boundaries between domains. Quarterly reviews. Indefinitely.

### Slide 10 — You can skip a phase — and pay for it several times over · 3:30–3:50 (20 s)

- The phases depend on each other, in order. Discover before you measure. Measure before you
  adapt. Adapt before you plan. Plan before you execute. Govern always.
- You can skip a phase, but you will pay for it later — usually several times what skipping
  appeared to save.

### Slide 11 — Week 1 to week 26 — then forever · 3:50–4:20 (30 s)

**This slide answers the retrieval question from Slide 2. Host B answers it explicitly:**

- The answer to the question from the start: six months. Week one, Discovery begins. Week
  twenty-six, an approved roadmap goes to cabinet. Then your country runs in permanent
  EA-governed mode.
- "Months, not years" is not a slogan. It is the consequence of five phases — each with a clear
  question, a clear deliverable, a clear sign-off — and the practice that runs after.

**Close on the series handoff, spoken by Host B:** the lifecycle tells you what the work is. The
next video covers what you will need from your minister to run it — four specific asks.

### Slide 12 — Sources · 4:20–4:30 (10 s)

One line only, then stop: "Sources are in the video description." **Do not read URLs. Do not
name section numbers. Do not summarise the sources.**

## 3. Hard prohibitions

- ❌ **No podcast outro.** No "that raises a fascinating question for you to consider", no "look
  around at the other institutions in your life", no invitation to reflect on other sectors. The
  audio ends on the series handoff in §2, Slide 11, plus the one-line sources note.
- ❌ **No "deep dive" self-reference.** Never say "deep dive", "today we're unpacking", "welcome
  to", "our sources", "the sources say", "here's where it gets interesting".
- ❌ **No invented specifics.** No named country, no cost figures, no percentages, no
  institutions beyond those in §2. The only durations permitted are the phase durations, "six
  months", "week one", "week twenty-six", and "quarterly" as given in §2.
- ❌ **No filler.** Remove "you know", "like", "I mean", "basically", "totally", "sort of",
  "right?", "wow", "oh absolutely", "man". Reaction interjections should be rare and short.
- ❌ **No consumer-outrage vocabulary.** Not "broken", "chaos", "held hostage", "extortionate",
  "nightmare". Say: fragmented, duplicated, costly to change, locked in.
- ❌ **No idioms or metaphors.** None is permitted in this video (§2). Much of this audience
  listens in English as an additional language and the audio is subtitled and translated.
- ❌ **No crosstalk or interruption.** One speaker finishes, the other begins. The audio is cut
  to slide transitions, so overlapping speech breaks the edit.
- ❌ **No in-video intro or outro branding.** ITU compliance rule — the video opens on content
  and closes on the sources line.
