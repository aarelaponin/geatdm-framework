**Description:**

```text
You are right to ask before you commit your agency. The core of it has already
been done — in countries large and small, unitary and federal, well-resourced and
not.

KP1 · Module 5 · 5.1 — Is this proven, or just theory? Evidence from real
programmes

Four governments that could hardly differ more in size, resources and shape:
Rwanda, small with a strong centre, one citizen-services platform and a national
identity linked across services; Kenya, one-stop centres and a unifying identity
programme, with results mixed and openly debated; South Africa, federal, so
nothing can be imposed from the top, coordinated instead through shared standards;
Estonia, distributed registries and the once-only principle. The same four
elements show up in all of them — and the failures are documented too, which is
exactly what makes it evidence rather than a brochure.
```

### 5.2 — What the evidence says works, and what kills these programmes

**File:** `video/KP1_M5_5.2_Video_v0.3.mp4` · **Subtitles:** `audio/KP1_M5_5.2_Audio_v0.3.srt`

**Title:** What quietly kills government digital programmes — usually in the second year

**Description:**

```text
What works is known: a protected team, a framework agencies adopt, governance that
can say no, funding sustained for years. The killers are the harder half — and
they are almost never technical.

KP1 · Module 5 · 5.2 — What the evidence says works, and what quietly kills these
programmes

Four things quietly kill these programmes, usually in the second year, and none of
them is dramatic. The team is pulled onto the urgent flagship and the architecture
work simply stops. The sponsor changes and the successor has their own priorities.
Governance drifts to advisory under delivery pressure — one powerful project
through, then another, until its "no" means nothing. Funding becomes an annual
favour, and one budget cycle removes it. All four are about people, authority and
money, which is the good news: those are the things you can influence, while you
still have the minister's attention.
```

**Description:**

```text
Does the method travel, and how do you roll it out? Two questions, and the answers
are linked.

KP1 · Module 5 · 5.3 — Roll it out across sectors, and why the second is cheaper

Most of the method does not change — only the record at the centre does: a
learner, a patient, a farmer, a beneficiary. Wave 1 looks like one sector, but
what it really builds is the national foundation: the permanent team, the
governance board, and the first shared platforms for identity and data exchange.
Every later sector stands on that, which is why Wave 2 costs less than Wave 1 and
Wave 4 is easier still — provided the same board governs the national pipeline.
Launched and left, a rollout drifts back into fragmentation; governed, it
compounds. Tracked on one page, every quarter.
```

### 5.4 — Win the commitment

**File:** `video/KP1_M5_5.4_Video_v0.4.mp4` · **Subtitles:** `audio/KP1_M5_5.4_Audio_v0.4.srt`

**Title:** Ministers don't commit to architecture — the business case that gets you to yes

**Description:**

```text
Slide a four-layer diagram across the cabinet table and watch the eyes glaze over.
Ministers do not commit to architecture. They commit to numbers, and to cases they
can defend in cabinet.

KP1 · Module 5 · 5.4 — Win the commitment: the business case that gets your
minister to yes

Lead with the saving, not the architecture: the strongest number you have is the
cost of fragmentation, because every programme that builds its own identity, its
own registry, its own integration means the country pays many times for one thing.
Back it with proof, because a minister's first worry is risk and a saving that
depends on an untested approach is easy to refuse. Then name the real cost and the
real horizon — a small permanent team, around two per cent of the digital budget
sustained for five years, six months to a roadmap and years to full maturity. Said
as a weakness, that sinks the case. Said as the truth, it wins it.
```

### 5.5 — Build your team's capability

**File:** `video/KP1_M5_5.5_Video_v0.6.mp4` · **Subtitles:** `audio/KP1_M5_5.5_Audio_v0.6.srt`

**Title:** You don't have to train your team from scratch — build capability with open knowledge

**Description:**

```text
Do we have the people, and can we afford to train them? You do not have to build
the knowledge from scratch — the method, the framework and the training materials
already exist as open knowledge products.

KP1 · Module 5 · 5.5 — Build your team's capability with open knowledge products

The method is not locked in a consultant's head any more: the reference
architecture is published, the building-block specifications are open, and the
knowledge products are made to teach a practising architect the method step by
step. Three layers you draw on rather than build — the published framework (what
to do), the worked examples and AI plays (how to do it faster), and the community
of countries and partners (who to ask when your team is stuck). So what you fund
is time and context, not invention: a small team given the time to learn it and
apply it to your country. Rent a consultancy and the expertise walks out when the
contract ends; upskill your own people and they stay.
```

### 5.6 — The closing case

**File:** `video/KP1_M5_5.6_Video_v0.2.mp4` · **Subtitles:** `audio/KP1_M5_5.6_Audio_v0.2.srt`

**Title:** Proven, portable, necessary now — the closing case for a national EA

**Description:**

```text
Three sentences a minister can hold. This is proven. It is portable. It is
necessary now. Underneath those sit the two reasons an Enterprise Architecture
exists at all.

KP1 · Module 5 · 5.6 — The closing case: proven, portable, and necessary now

Proven, because four very different governments built the same pattern and both
the successes and the failures are documented. Portable, because the method is
sector-agnostic and each sector after the first costs less. And necessary now,
because for thirty years digital government meant putting paper online — the form
became a web form, the queue an appointment — and the work that delivers today is
different: once-only data sharing, a shared identity, a single record that follows
a person across services. That cannot be done by the IT department alone or the
policy side alone, which is where the two reasons come in: an EA is what makes
re-use rational, and what gives business and IT a shared language.

The closing video of Knowledge Product 1.
```
