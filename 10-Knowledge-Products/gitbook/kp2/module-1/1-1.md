# 1.1 Why interoperability can't be bought, only built

> **Single message.** Procurement rules can require interoperability. Only whole-of-government planning delivers it — and once the framework exists, procurement is how you enforce it.

## The concept

Your government has probably already required interoperability. Every new system contract says the supplier must provide open interfaces. The national digital strategy says systems must connect. And yet your health ministry still records the citizen's ID number on paper, and your school system records it again, on paper, at enrolment. The requirement is written down. The result is not there. The question this video answers is why — and what actually works instead.

Here is the uncomfortable truth. A procurement rule can require a behaviour. It cannot make that behaviour the cheapest choice for the team doing the work. Inside any one project, the team has a budget and a deadline. Connecting to another ministry's system means learning that system, negotiating with that ministry's people, and waiting for their timelines. Building a quick point-to-point link of their own is faster. So they build their own. That is not indiscipline. That is the project doing exactly what it was funded to do.

The result is point-to-point sprawl. Last year your team connected the tax office to the business register, and built that connection from scratch. This year the health ministry needs the same data, and the connection is built again, from scratch. Each link is private, undocumented, and breaks when either side changes. Nobody planned the shared road that every one of these journeys should travel on. There was no one whose job it was to plan it.

Interoperability becomes the rational choice only from one vantage point — the whole of government. From there the maths changes. The first agency pays to build a shared way to exchange data. The second agency does not build its own; it connects to the shared one. The third and fourth do the same. The country builds the road once, and every agency reuses it. But this view does not exist inside any single project. It exists only at the level of your country's whole digital portfolio — and someone has to hold it deliberately. That is what an interoperability framework is for.

A framework is the plan, made of four parts that a contract clause cannot deliver. A legal mandate that makes data exchange lawful and connection mandatory. An organisational layer that names who owns the bus, who may join, and who is accountable. A semantic layer so that two agencies mean the same thing by the word 'learner'. And a technical layer — the shared bus and the standards every member uses. This knowledge product builds all four, in order, for a real exchange. The point here is that no procurement clause produces any of them. Only planning at the level of the whole government does.

None of this means you stop procuring. You will still buy — the central platform, the security servers, the integration services. What the framework changes is what the tender says. Once the framework exists, its reference architecture is the specification, its rules go into every new tender as mandatory requirements, and the country owns the architecture rather than inheriting whichever one the winning vendor brought. And the platform itself is bought as a working, accepted result — with acceptance tests, a warranty and penalties for delay — not as a consulting exercise. So the sequence is the whole lesson: plan the framework first, then let procurement enforce it. The toolkit behind this knowledge product carries that path, from reference architecture to an issuable tender.

Country: Progressa. The description, taken from A0 §1 and §8:

Interoperability is required in three places. The Public Procurement Authority's standard ICT tender documents ask every supplier to provide "open, documented interfaces". The 2021 e-Government Interoperability Framework names message formats and an approved-standards list, but its governance committee no longer meets and no current project cites it. The draft Digital Transformation Roadmap 2024–2030 lists once-only and open standards among its eight principles; it awaits cabinet, and PDGA's mandate coordinates rather than binds.

Linkup, an X-Road 7.x layer run by PDGA, has been in pilot since 2025 with four members: PNIA, the business register, the tax authority and PDGA. No service catalogue is published. The tax-to-business-register link is a direct database link built in 2022 and never migrated. The Social Protection Agency's beneficiary register (2016) is maintained only by the vendor that built it. MoEYS and Health exchange data by spreadsheet on request. Learners prove identity and enrolment on paper at every counter. PayPro exists; the scholarship programme pays by cheque.

{% tab title="Reading the output" %}
This is the teaching part of the play. Work through the output the way a chief architect would — what is earned, what is invented, what is missing.

<details>

<summary>1. The required-versus-delivered gap is earned</summary>

Every "required" row traces to a document in the input: the tender clause, the 2021 framework, the draft Roadmap. Every "delivered" row traces to a system or to paper. The table does not claim Linkup delivers any exchange. It marks the bus as named but uncatalogued, which is exactly what the prompt's "be conservative" rule asks for.

</details>

<details>

<summary>2. The tender clause is an invented detail</summary>

The Progressa pack does not quote a procurement clause on interfaces; the example input supplied one to give the play something to test. If your country has no such clause, the honest row says so, and the argument changes: interoperability is not even required, let alone delivered.

</details>

<details>

<summary>3. The risk column is guesswork on the bus row</summary>

"Low, the bus is PDGA's" assumes PDGA can run Linkup without a supplier. The input does not say who operates the pilot day to day. Ask PDGA who holds the operating contract before the row goes into a cabinet paper.

</details>

<details>

<summary>4. What to check next: what actually runs on Linkup</summary>

Four members is not four exchanges. Ask PDGA for the list of services live on the pilot and who calls them. Until that list exists, the bus is plumbing, not yet a result.

</details>
