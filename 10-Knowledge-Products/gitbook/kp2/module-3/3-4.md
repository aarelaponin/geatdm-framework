## The concept

Joining the bus is not just plugging in a cable. It is accepting a set of obligations. A federation where each member picks and chooses which rules to follow is a free-for-all that no one can depend on — and the first time a member's stale data corrupts another agency's service, the whole framework loses trust. The member obligations, written down and signed, are what turn a collection of connections into a dependable federation.

Every member accepts a common set of obligations. Connect, and stay connected — not drop off when attention moves elsewhere. Meet the agreed service levels — uptime, response time. Keep its data accurate and current, because a registry that is stale poisons every exchange that relies on it. Answer the queries it is obliged to, so that once-only actually works for the citizen. Protect the data it receives. And follow the standards the framework sets, rather than its own. Two more that are easy to forget and expensive to skip: name two people — a Technical Focal Point the operator can call, and a Data Protection Officer wherever personal data flows — and pay its share of the running cost, where the Council decides that members contribute. Eight obligations, the same for everyone.

But the obligations are not identical on both sides, and naming the difference is important for fairness. The agency that provides data carries more weight — the load on its systems, the duty to keep the data accurate, the availability its consumers depend on. The agency that consumes data carries obligations of purpose and protection — use the data only for the agreed purpose, protect it, do not re-share it. A fair member agreement recognises that the provider is being asked to do more, and that the Operating Authority and the service levels exist partly to support it. Ignore this, and provider agencies quietly resist joining.

All of this becomes a member agreement — the document a CIO signs when their agency joins the bus. Putting the obligations in a signed agreement turns them from an assumption into a commitment, and gives the Operating Authority something concrete to point to when a member stops meeting them. The member agreement is the organisational-layer counterpart to the decree: the decree compels connection in law; the agreement records what each member commits to in practice.

**Linkup Member Agreement (draft)** — between PDGA, as Operating Authority, and the member agency.

**1. What every member commits to.** Connect and stay connected. Meet the agreed service levels. Keep its data accurate and current. Answer the queries the framework obliges it to answer, so a citizen gives data once. Protect what it receives. Follow Linkup's published standards. Name a Technical Focal Point and, wherever personal data flows, a Data Protection Officer. Pay any contribution the Council sets; none is set for the pilot phase **[confirm with the Council]**.

**2. If you provide data (PNIA, PLR).** Keep your service available at 99.5% a month. Correct a reported error within five working days. Give five working days' notice of a planned change. Carry the query load the catalogue entry states.

**3. If you consume data (PNEA).** Use the data only for the purpose in the catalogue entry: confirming a candidate's identity and enrolment. Do not store more than you need. Do not pass it on to any other body.

**4. What the framework gives a provider in return.** The operator monitors your service and handles first-line support for consumers. Service levels are agreed with you, not for you. The Steering Committee refuses an exchange that would overload you.

**5. Breach.** A breach is missing a service level three months running, an unanswered obligatory query, or any use beyond the stated purpose. The operator writes to the member and agrees a fix within 30 days. If the breach persists, the regulator may suspend access **[confirm: depends on the decree]**.

Signed: Head of the member agency; Director-General, PDGA.

{% tab title="Reading the output" %}
This is the teaching part of the play. Work through the output the way a chief architect would — what is earned, what is invented, what is missing.

<details>

<summary>1. The provider-consumer balance is earned</summary>

Section 4 is what makes PNIA likely to sign: it names what PNIA gets for carrying the load. The input made PNIA the only champion, and the agreement treats it as the agency most worth keeping. Keep section 4 when you edit; it is the first thing a provider's CIO reads.

</details>

<details>

<summary>2. The 99.5% was set for the providers, not with them</summary>

The figure came from the input, and it matches the signed service levels in the build pack. But the safeguard says to agree the number with each provider. PNIA's e-KYC runs today and can say what it meets; PLR is still being established and cannot yet promise anything. Ask before you print it.

</details>

<details>

<summary>3. Minors' data needs more than "purpose limitation"</summary>

The consumer section names the purpose but not the legal basis. Every record in the first exchange is a learner's, and most learners are minors. Add a line: the consumer states the statutory basis for each query, and the Data Protection Officers of both members confirm it before the first call.

</details>

<details>

<summary>4. There is no way out</summary>

The agreement says how to join and how to be suspended, not how to leave. Add an exit clause: notice period, removal from the catalogue, and how long message logs are kept after a member leaves.

</details>

{% hint style="danger" %}
**Safeguard for this play.** Enforcement powers — suspending or sanctioning a member — usually derive from the decree, not the agreement alone; mark any such power as [confirm] against the legal layer. And set service-level numbers with the provider agencies, not for them, or the agreement will be signed and then ignored.
