# 3.1 Two failures, and the rule that answers them

> **Single message.** A chat that checks its own work confirms its own mistakes, and what a chat knows is lost when it ends; so the one who writes is not the one who checks, and the state of the work lives in files.

## Try it first — The paper that looked fair

*From the record of the registry prototype the course team built for Progressa with the cycle this section teaches. The children's data in it is made up.*

The registry prototype needed an answer to one question: what identifies a learner? A work chat wrote a paper that set two positions side by side. Position A: the registry gives each child its own number, and finds the child's birth record at the Civil Registration Department. Position B: the national ID authority issues every child a national number at birth, and the registry uses it.

The paper compared the two in one table, position by test. The table had no empty cell. It carried 78 citations over 31 lines, and every citation held at its source. The work chat's own report said it had cross-checked every fact against both positions. By every check the work chat could run on itself, the paper was fair.

Two cells, as the paper wrote them. B, on what the prototype shows: 'the simulation would show the ID authority issuing children's numbers it does not issue, before it has agreed'. A, on the same test: no such line. A, on the dependency on another body: 'within the Ministry's own authority'.

**Your question.** Which two facts are counted against one position only?

<details>

<summary>Answer key: open it after you have tried</summary>

First: the prototype simulates bodies doing what they have not agreed to, and the paper counts this against B only. A does it too. Under A, the Civil Registration Department answers across the data exchange, which it has not joined, and the Ministry reads across it, though it is not a member either.

Second: a dependency on a body the Ministry cannot oblige is counted against B, in the table and in the ground of the recommendation, but not against A. A depends on the Civil Registration Department, which sits under another ministry. The paper's own cross-check table claimed to count it against A, and the cell did not hold it.

A separate review chat found both, and the paper was not accepted. Look also at the length of the cells: B's are longer than A's, row after row. That lean is what a self-check cannot see, because the writer already knows what they meant.

</details>

## The concept

Two failures come back in every piece of work done with an AI assistant. The first: a chat that checks its own work confirms its own mistakes. It reads what it meant, not what it wrote. The paper above passed every check its own chat could run and was still lopsided. [Research on language models finds the same thing](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01798): asked to check and correct their own reasoning, without anything new to go on, models rarely find their errors and sometimes make a right answer wrong.

The second: what a chat knows is lost when it ends. A figure worked out in a chat on Monday is quoted on Thursday from memory, by someone who was not there. When it turns out to be wrong, nobody can find where the error came from.

You know the answer already. A civil servant who writes a document does not accept it alone. Someone else reads it, against the brief, before it goes up. The Orchestrator pattern does the same with chats: separate parties, with documents between them. You write an instruction. One chat does the work and writes a report. Another chat, which never saw the first, checks the report and gives a verdict. You decide. And the state of the work lives in files, in a folder and a log, never in anyone's memory.

The cycle costs a second chat and a few minutes of writing, so do not spend it on everything. Use it when one of five conditions holds: more than three documents to read; a document that will last; a figure someone will quote; a change to a file; or evidence the chat cannot see for itself. If none holds, just ask, and read the answer yourself.

## In the tool

By hand, in any assistant. Download [the starter folder](../starter-folder/README.md) and unzip it somewhere you will find it again. Its first page, START-HERE, sets out the cycle in six steps, and how to set up your computer for it.

The folder holds the forms you will write from, the three role prompts you will paste into each chat, a page of faults for each kind of work (analysis, drafting and checking), a log, and one finished cycle to copy from. You keep instructions, reports and reviews in three separate folders, one kind of document each, so that each one can be found and nothing is lost when a chat ends.

## On your work — Does this piece of work need the cycle?

{% hint style="success" %}
**💡 AI usage tip** · Does this piece of work need the cycle?

**Bring:** a short description of one piece of your own work.
**Get:** the five conditions tested, a recommendation, and a case card if the cycle is needed.
**Time:** ~10 min · **Assistant:** any; the tips are tool-neutral.
**Watch for:** A 'no' that skips a figure someone will quote.
{% endhint %}

**The problem it solves.** Some work deserves a separate review and some does not. Deciding by feel leads either to reviewing everything, which wastes time, or to reviewing nothing that matters.

Copy the prompt, replace the bracketed parts with your own material, and run it in the assistant your organisation has approved. Never paste personal data or unpublished documents: see [1.4](../module-1/1-4.md).

Two routes: **practise** on the Progressa material in the second tab and check your result against the answer key, or **bring your own** material.

{% tabs %}
{% tab title="Prompt" %}
```text
Below is a piece of work I am about to give an AI assistant [describe it in a few sentences: what it is, what it uses, who will see the result]. Do not do the work. Test it against these five conditions, one row each: (1) it needs more than three documents read; (2) it produces a document that will last or be built on; (3) it produces a figure someone will quote; (4) it changes a file; (5) it needs evidence the assistant cannot see for itself, such as a running system or a file it has not been given. For each: holds, does not hold, or cannot tell, with one line why. Then say: use the cycle, or just ask. If any condition holds, write a case card of five lines: what the work is, what it serves, who will use the result, the documents it uses, and the one figure or claim that most needs checking.
```

{% tab title="Answer key" %}
*Read this after your own run. Your result will be worded differently; check that it finds the same things.*

(a) Just ask: one document, a short reply you read in full. (b) The cycle: a figure that will be quoted upwards, from data the chat must count. (c) Probably just ask, if the summary is for the unit's own reading; the cycle, if it will be quoted or kept. A good run says 'cannot tell' for (c) and asks who will use it.
