# 1.1 A drafting partner, not an oracle

> **Single message.** An assistant writes a wrong answer as fluently as a right one, and tends to agree with you, so how well a draft reads tells you nothing about whether it is right.

## The concept

An AI assistant writes a strong first draft in minutes: a briefing note, a terms of reference, a comparison of options. It does not write a finding you can trust without checking. A wrong section number, an invented figure and a claim with no basis come out in exactly the same confident tone as a correct one.

The next, better model will not remove this. The US standards body [NIST lists it among the risks of generative AI](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.600-1.pdf) and calls it confabulation: false content stated with confidence. One cause is the way assistants are trained and scored. The tests the industry uses reward a confident guess over the words 'I do not know', so a system that always answers scores better than one that admits a gap. [OpenAI, one of the vendors, explained this in 2025](https://openai.com/index/why-language-models-hallucinate/). For a public servant it means that fluency is not evidence: a smooth paragraph and a correct one look the same.

An assistant also tends to accept the framing of your question. Ask why your data problem is severe, and you get reasons it is severe. Ask why it is minor, and you get reasons it is minor. [OpenAI described shipping a model that flattered its users](https://openai.com/index/expanding-on-sycophancy/) this way and having to withdraw it. The same habit makes 'are you sure?' a weak check: the assistant tends to agree that it is sure, or to agree that it was wrong, whichever your question suggests.

Working with an assistant also changes what people conclude. In [a field experiment with 758 consultants of the Boston Consulting Group](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=64700), those given an assistant did better on tasks inside what it does well. On a task chosen to lie just outside it, they were 19% less likely to reach the right answer than colleagues working without one: the assistant's fluent answer took the place of their own judgement.

What this costs when nobody checks is on public record. [A database kept since 2023](https://www.damiencharlotin.com/hallucinations/) lists 2,217 court decisions worldwide, as at 25 October 2026, in which a lawyer filed an invented citation or a made-up quotation produced by an assistant. These are professionals with a duty of care, filing documents they did not check. A government programme has the same risk: a country that did not do what the briefing says, a clause of a standard that does not exist, a figure with no source.

So use the assistant as a drafting partner. It is fast, tireless and good with structure. Give it your material, take its draft, and then check every claim that matters against a named source before the draft leaves your desk.

## In the tool

Shown in Claude. Two settings change how far you can trust a draft, and both are next to the box where you type.

The model. Assistants offer more than one model. A larger model reasons more carefully and is slower; a smaller one is quicker and good for short, routine drafting. Choose the larger one for analysis, a comparison or anything with figures. Choosing a larger model makes errors rarer. It does not remove them, and it does not change what you must check.

Search and citations. With web search turned on, the assistant looks up current pages and marks each claim it took from one with a citation you can open. With search off, it answers from what it learned in training, which stops at a date and carries no sources. Turn search on for any question about what a country, a body or a standard did or says. Then open the citations: a citation proves only that a page exists, not that it says what the draft claims.

## Worked example — A law it was never shown

*Progressa is the fictional country every course in this knowledge base uses; every body, figure and date in the example is invented. MoEYS is its Ministry of Education, Youth and Skills; PNEA its National Examinations Authority; PNIA its National ID Authority; PDGA its Digital Government Authority; and the PLR the Progressa Learner Registry it plans.*

The ICT Director of MoEYS, Progressa's ministry of education, asked an assistant, with search off: 'Summarise what Progressa's Data Protection Act 2023 requires before we share learner data with another ministry.' The answer was clear and well set out. It said that section 14(3) of the Act requires a data-sharing agreement approved by the Data Protection Commission, that a breach must be reported within 72 hours, and that learners over 16 may consent for themselves.

None of the three came from the Act. The Director had not pasted it, and the assistant could not have read it. The Act, when the Director opened it, says two things on the point: a legal basis is needed to share a minor's data, and parental consent is needed for any use the law does not require. There is no section 14(3) on data sharing, and the Act sets no breach deadline.

The second run pasted the Act's text and asked for the obligations, each with the words of the Act quoted beside it. The answer was shorter, less impressive and right. The first draft had sounded more expert because no source limited it.

## Your turn — List the claims in a draft that need a source

{% hint style="success" %}
**💡 AI usage tip** · List the claims in a draft that need a source

**Bring:** the draft, as written.
**Get:** a table of the claims in the draft that need a source, with where to check each one and what goes wrong if it is false.
**Time:** ~10 min · **Assistant:** any; the tips are tool-neutral.
**Watch for:** The table lists the claims; it checks none of them.
{% endhint %}

**The problem it solves.** A draft from an assistant reads well, and the claims in it that need checking are hidden among the sentences that do not. Before the draft goes into a briefing, someone has to find every figure, date, name, legal reference and citation, and say how each will be checked.

Copy the prompt, replace the bracketed parts with your own material, and run it in the assistant your organisation has approved. Never paste personal data or unpublished documents: see [1.4](../module-1/1-4.md).

Two routes: **practise** on the Progressa material in the second tab and check your result against the answer key, or **bring your own** material.

{% tabs %}
{% tab title="Prompt" %}
```text
Below is a draft written with an AI assistant, which I intend to use in [a briefing note, a report, a letter] for [the reader] [paste the draft]. List every claim in it that a reader could check: figures, dates, names of bodies, legal or policy references, quotations, and statements about what a country, body or standard did or requires. For each claim produce one row with: (a) the claim, quoted word for word; (b) its kind: figure, date, name, legal reference, quotation or statement of fact; (c) the source the draft gives for it, or 'none'; (d) where I could check it: the document, body or system that would hold the answer; (e) what goes wrong if it is false, in one line. Do not judge whether any claim is true, and do not add claims the draft does not make. Output: a table with one row per claim, then the number of claims with no source.
```
{% endtab %}

A draft note, written with an assistant, for the Permanent Secretary of MoEYS: Sharing learner data with the examinations authority.

(1) The Data Protection Act 2023 needs a legal basis before a minor's data is shared, and a parent's consent for any use the law does not require. (2) Section 22 of the Act allows ministries to share learner data with each other without consent. (3) Linkup, the national data-exchange layer, has run as a pilot since 2025 with four members; MoEYS is not one of them. (4) MoEYS will join Linkup in 2027. (5) PNIA issues national IDs to children from the age of 12 (PNIA annual report 2025). (6) Birth registration stands at 71%. (7) About 40% of learner records carry a misspelt name. (8) The Data Protection Commission fined a ministry in 2025 for sharing learner data. (9) The Education Sector Plan 2023–2028 calls for the Progressa Learner Registry (PLR), which has not started.

{% endtab %}

{% tab title="Answer key" %}
*Read this after your own run. Your result will be worded differently; check that it finds the same things.*

The table should hold nine rows, one per numbered sentence, and say that eight have no source: only sentence 5 names one. The main kind of each: 1 and 2 legal references; 3, 5, 6 and 7 figures; 4 a date; 8 and 9 statements of fact. Some hold more than one kind: 3 and 8 also hold a date, and 9 names a plan.

Checked against [the Progressa page](../../start-here/progressa.md) of this knowledge base, sentences 1, 3, 6 and 9 hold. Five do not, and they are the kinds this page warns of: (2) an invented section number, the Act allows no such thing; (4) a date the draft inferred and states as fact, no plan names one; (5) a wrong figure behind a real-looking source, PNIA issues IDs at 16; (7) a figure with no source at all; (8) an event that did not happen, the Commission has taken no enforcement action.

If your table marked any of the nine as true or false, the assistant went beyond the prompt: the tip lists claims, it does not judge them.

{% endtab %}
{% endtabs %}

{% hint style="danger" %}
**Safeguard.** The table lists claims to check; it does not check them, and it can miss a claim. Read the draft yourself as well, and check each row against the source it names, never by asking an assistant whether the claim is true: it tends to agree with the way you ask.
{% endhint %}
