# 1.3 Read the output before you use it

> **Single message.** Before you use a draft, find what it rests on: what the source states, what the assistant inferred, and what it should have asked you.

## The concept

A draft from an assistant mixes three kinds of sentence, and they look the same. Some state what your source says. Some are inferred: the assistant drew a conclusion the source does not state. And some come from nowhere: plausible sentences that fill a gap with something nobody supplied. Reading the output means telling these apart before the draft is used.

Ask for the open questions. A good draft on a hard subject says what it could not settle: a figure it did not have, a decision nobody has taken. Ask the assistant to list them at the end of every draft. And be wary of a draft with no open questions at all. On a subject your unit has never settled, that is a warning sign: it usually means the assistant answered the hard questions itself.

Cite or discard. Any claim about the outside world, such as what a country did or what a standard requires, needs a source you can open. Assistants invent citations as fluently as they write prose. Open the source. If it does not say what the draft claims, discard the claim, not only the citation: a claim whose source has gone is a guess again.

Anthropic's [AI Fluency course](https://academy.claude.com/courses/ai-fluency-framework-foundations) calls this skill discernment: judging how useful an output is. It is the step people skip, because the draft already looks finished. A [Microsoft Research survey of 319 knowledge workers](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/the-impact-of-generative-ai-on-critical-thinking-self-reported-reductions-in-cognitive-effort-and-confidence-effects-from-a-survey-of-knowledge-workers/) found that the more they trusted the assistant, the less they checked its output critically. [The Government of Canada tells its public servants the same](https://www.canada.ca/en/government/system/digital-government/digital-government-innovations/responsible-use-ai/guide-use-generative-ai.html): do not treat an output as authoritative, and do not use an assistant as a search engine unless it gives sources you can check. A minute spent reading against the source is cheaper than a correction sent after the briefing.

One more check, where your work crosses languages. Assistants may be weaker in languages with less text online. If a draft is translated, or written in a local language, have a colleague who speaks it fluently read it before it is used.

## In the tool

Shown in Claude.

Opening the sources. When search is on, each claim taken from a web page carries a citation. Click it, and find the sentence in the page that supports the claim. A page on the right subject that does not say the thing is not a source for it.

Asking what it inferred. Add one line to the end of a prompt, or send it after the draft: 'Mark each sentence as stated in the source, inferred, or not in the source, and list the questions you should have asked me.' Then read the sentences marked inferred and not in the source first.

Using a second chat. To have a draft checked, open a new chat and paste the draft and its source there. A chat that did not write the draft has no draft of its own to defend.

**Where this is in each assistant:** [Claude: web search](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/10684626-enable-and-use-web-search) · [ChatGPT: search](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/9237897-chatgpt-search) · [Gemini: sources](https://support.google.com/gemini/answer/14143489) · [Microsoft 365 Copilot: web search](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-365/copilot/manage-public-web-access)

## Worked example — A gap note, marked

*Progressa is the fictional country every course in this knowledge base uses; every body, figure and date in the example is invented. MoEYS is its Ministry of Education, Youth and Skills; PNEA its National Examinations Authority; PNIA its National ID Authority; PDGA its Digital Government Authority; and the PLR the Progressa Learner Registry it plans.*

An analyst of PDGA, Progressa's digital government authority, asked an assistant to read the draft Digital Transformation Roadmap 2024–2030 and write a one-page note on its gaps for data exchange. The note was twelve sentences long and had no open questions.

The analyst sent the marking line. Eight sentences came back as stated, each with a quotation. Three came back as inferred, among them 'the Roadmap assumes every ministry will join Linkup by 2027', which the Roadmap does not say. One was not in the source: 'the 2021 e-Government Interoperability Framework requires all exchanges to use X-Road.' The analyst opened the 2021 framework. It names message formats and a list of approved standards. It does not name X-Road.

The analyst discarded that sentence, turned the inferred 2027 date into a question for the Roadmap's owner, and asked the assistant again for the open questions. It listed four, among them who decides which ministries join Linkup first. The final note was ten sentences and four questions, and every sentence in it could be traced.

## Your turn — Mark what a draft rests on

{% hint style="success" %}
**💡 AI usage tip** · Mark what a draft rests on

**Bring:** the source document and the draft written from it.
**Get:** a table of the draft's sentences, each marked stated, inferred or not in the source, with the questions the draft should have raised.
**Time:** ~15 min · **Assistant:** any; the tips are tool-neutral.
**Watch for:** Quotations the source does not hold.
{% endhint %}

**The problem it solves.** A draft has been written from a source, but nobody can tell which of its sentences the source supports, which the assistant inferred, and which came from nowhere. Until that is known, the draft cannot be signed or sent.

Copy the prompt, replace the bracketed parts with your own material, and run it in the assistant your organisation has approved. Never paste personal data or unpublished documents: see [1.4](../module-1/1-4.md).

Two routes: **practise** on the Progressa material in the second tab and check your result against the answer key, or **bring your own** material.

{% tabs %}
{% tab title="Prompt" %}
```text
Below is a source document [paste the source], and below that a draft written from it [paste the draft]. For each sentence of the draft, produce one row with: (a) the sentence, word for word; (b) its standing: 'stated' if the source says it, 'inferred' if it is a conclusion the source does not state, or 'not in the source'; (c) for 'stated', the words of the source that say it, quoted exactly; for 'inferred', the passage it was inferred from. Then list the questions the draft should have raised and did not: the points on which the source is silent or unclear, and the decision each one needs. Do not correct the draft. Output: the table, then the list of questions, then the number of sentences of each standing.
```
{% endtab %}

The source: an extract of [the Progressa page](../../start-here/progressa.md) of this knowledge base. The Data Protection Act 2023 requires a legal basis for sharing minors' data and parental consent for any use the law does not require. Linkup, the national data-exchange layer, went live in 2025 in pilot with four members: PNIA, the business register, the tax authority and PDGA; MoEYS is not a member. PNIA covers 78% of adults but issues child IDs only at 16; birth registration is at 71%. The Data Protection Commission was established in 2023, has 6 staff and has taken no enforcement action yet. The Education Sector Plan 2023–2028 calls for the Progressa Learner Registry (PLR), not yet started. The same child may appear in a district file, a provincial secondary file and the examinations authority's candidate list with different spellings.

The draft: a note for the Permanent Secretary of MoEYS. (1) The Data Protection Act 2023 needs a legal basis before a minor's data is shared, and a parent's consent for any use the law does not require. (2) Section 22 of the Act allows ministries to share learner data with each other without consent. (3) Linkup, the national data-exchange layer, has run as a pilot since 2025 with four members; MoEYS is not one of them. (4) MoEYS will join Linkup in 2027. (5) PNIA issues national IDs to children from the age of 12 (PNIA annual report 2025). (6) Birth registration stands at 71%. (7) About 40% of learner records carry a misspelt name. (8) The Data Protection Commission fined a ministry in 2025 for sharing learner data. (9) The Education Sector Plan 2023–2028 calls for the Progressa Learner Registry (PLR), which has not started.

| Sentence | Standing | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1, 3, 6, 9 | stated | Each is in the source, in other words |
| 2 | not in the source | The source names no section, and no exception to consent |
| 4 | not in the source | The source says MoEYS is not a member; no date is given anywhere |
| 5 | not in the source | The source says 16, and cites no annual report |
| 7 | not in the source | The source says spellings differ; it gives no figure. An assistant often marks this one 'inferred' |
| 8 | not in the source | The source says the Commission has taken no enforcement action |

Questions the draft should have raised: which legal basis applies to sharing with the examinations authority; whether that authority would need to join Linkup; how consent works for a learner with no birth record.

If your run marked 7 as 'stated', find the quotation it gave in the source: it is not there. That is the safeguard's point.

{% hint style="danger" %}
**Safeguard.** An assistant marking a draft tends to be generous to it, and can quote words the source does not hold. Find each quotation in the source yourself, with the search of your document reader, before you rely on a sentence marked 'stated'. Run the tip in a new chat, not the one that wrote the draft.
{% endhint %}
