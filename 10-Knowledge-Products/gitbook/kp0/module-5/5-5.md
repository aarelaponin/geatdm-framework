# 5.5 Judgement: what a toy proves

> **Single message.** A prototype is for agreement: it shows that something can work, never that it is ready, and you decide when it has shown enough.

## Try it first: What the note may say

*From the record of the registry prototype, which [5.1](5-1.md) introduces.*

The full-size prototype's accepted note to the Permanent Secretary opens: 'It is a prototype for agreement … it is not the registry itself.' Here are five sentences someone might want to write about it.


| # | Sentence |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | The registry will stop children being registered twice. |
| 2 | The search finds a registered child when the family name, given names and date of birth match, capitals aside. |
| 3 | The examinations authority receives only the fields its basis allows. |
| 4 | The prototype is ready to be piloted in three schools. |
| 5 | Every basis for sharing a learner's data is the prototype's assumption, for the Data Protection Commission to confirm. |

**Your question.** Which of the five can the record support, and what is wrong with the others?

<details>

<summary>Answer key: open it after you have tried</summary>

2 and 5, as written; 3 only with its limits: on two learners, and never against a national number, which no invented learner holds.

1 claims the whole registry from one step, the search, which misses a child spelt differently; whether anything else finds the child was not shown. 4 is a decision, and not the prototype's: whether to pilot is the owner's ruling, and nothing in the record tested a school, real data or a real connection to another body.

</details>

## The concept

A prototype is for agreement. It shows the people who decide that something can work, on made-up data, on one computer, with the other bodies simulated. It never shows that the thing is ready: not with real data, not under load, not with a real connection, not with the law confirmed. Say so on the first page of anything you write about it.

Know what it cost. The full-size prototype took 23 pieces of work over five days: 13 accepted at the first version, and 10 that needed a second attempt, 2 of which were closed by changing what was asked, not by being accepted. The toy was planned as eight rounds and took 17, in one day of about nine hours: the brief's eight, three papers putting open questions to you, four rounds saving the work in the project's history, one moving the log under that history, and one bringing a page of faults up to date. Nine were accepted at the first version and five at the second; three were withdrawn after a second version not accepted, and ruled on directly. Each round costs a few minutes of your reading and ruling, and the review costs a second session. That is cheap next to a design accepted without a check, and costly next to a question you could have asked in one chat. [Böckeler found the same](https://martinfowler.com/articles/exploring-gen-ai/sdd-3-tools.html) with the spec-driven tools she tried: one turned a small bug into four user stories with sixteen tests of acceptance, 'a sledgehammer to crack a nut'. A way of working sized for a prototype is the wrong one for a small change. The five conditions of Module 3 tell you when to use it.

You decide when it has shown enough. Stop when the question it was built to answer is answered, and the note says what it shows and what it does not. A prototype that keeps growing is turning into the system without anyone having decided to build one.

Then take what it showed to where the real thing is built. In government, a service is specified and built the way the service design course teaches: from the agreed requirements and design, by the people and tools the ministry has chosen. The prototype's documents, the register, the groundwork and the goal written in full, travel; its code does not.

[Watch for cognitive debt](https://margaretstorey.com/blog/2026/02/09/cognitive-debt/) here more than anywhere: a prototype that works on screen is easy to accept without being able to say why it is right. Before your last ruling, say in your own words what each round found. Where you cannot, ask the orchestrator to explain until you can.

## On your work: Decide whether the prototype has shown enough

{% hint style="success" %}
**💡 AI usage tip** · Decide whether the prototype has shown enough

**Bring:** the brief, the log and the note.
**Get:** whether the question is answered, what the limits mean for the decision, what travels, and a recommendation to stop or to run one more round.
**Time:** ~15 min · **Assistant:** any; the tips are tool-neutral.
**Watch for:** A next step that grows the prototype instead of answering the question.
{% endhint %}

**The problem it solves.** When a prototype works, the next step suggests itself: one more feature. Deciding whether it has answered its question, and what goes forward, is the owner's last and most important ruling.

Copy the prompt, replace the bracketed parts with your own material, and run it in the assistant your organisation has approved. Never paste personal data or unpublished documents: see [1.4](../module-1/1-4.md).

Two routes: **practise** on the Progressa material in the second tab and check your result against the answer key, or **bring your own** material.

{% tabs %}
{% tab title="Prompt" %}
```text
Below are my prototype's brief [paste it], its log [paste it] and its note [paste it]. Do not propose new features. (1) State the question the brief says the prototype was built to answer, in one sentence. (2) Say whether the note answers it, quoting the sentences that do. (3) List what the note says the prototype does not show, and for each, whether it matters for the decision the brief serves. (4) List the documents that should travel to the people who build the real thing, and what should not. Output: four short sections, then one line: stop here, or the one further round that would answer the question better, with its reason.
```
{% endtab %}

*Invented for this course: every name, number and date below is fictional. Paste it where the prompt asks for your material.*

The mini registry's brief, its log of 17 rounds, each closed by a ruling, and its note, which says: the search finds 4 of the 8 children on more than one list from every list and misses each of the other 4 on the list where the family name is spelt differently; whether anything else would find them is not shown; all data is made up; nothing is connected to another body.

{% endtab %}

{% tab title="Answer key" %}
*Read this after your own run. Your result will be worded differently; check that it finds the same things.*

The question was whether a clerk can enrol a child without the registry registering the same child twice. The note answers it in part: yes for a child spelt the same on every list, no for a child spelt differently. That limit matters for the decision, so the honest next step is a question for the planning unit, whether the search should find names spelt differently, not a new feature. What travels: the register, the groundwork, the goal in full, and the note; not the code. A good run recommends stopping, with that question put to the owner.
