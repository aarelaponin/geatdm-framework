# 4.2 No list longer than nine: choose by category

{% hint style="info" %}

The page below does not depend on the video: it carries what the video will say. The [video index](../../start-here/video-index.md) is the page to watch for links.
{% endhint %}

> **Single message.** A long list is divided into categories, and the officer picks the category first.

## The concept

Ask an officer to pick one value from a list of twenty, and she scrolls, reads, and sometimes picks the neighbour. The method has a plain rule for this, and it costs nothing to apply while the application is designed.

No step of a choice offers more than nine values, and seven is the aim. A longer list is divided into categories, each holding no more than nine values. The officer picks the category first, then the value inside it. If a list ever needs more than nine categories, another level is added above them. The categories are a list like any other: they have names, someone keeps them, and each new value is placed in one of them when it is added.

Why should this matter to you? An officer reads a short list at a glance and finds her value. A long list must be scrolled, and the wrong value is always one line away. A wrong value chosen on a busy day sends a file to the wrong desk, and nobody notices until someone asks where it went. Two short choices are faster and safer than one long one, and the officers who use the screens every day will tell you so.

Who decides the categories? Not a program. A program can count the values, but it cannot know how your officers think about them. So the categories of each long list are decided once, in the interaction design, the one document that settles how officers use the whole application. The analyst proposes them, and the officers who use the list confirm that each value sits where they would look for it. After that, the program that checks the application's description refuses a long list with no categories.

Here is the one long list in the application of MoEYS, Progressa's ministry of education. PHEQA, the quality authority, advises the minister on twenty kinds of matter, from a provisional licence for a university to a change of an institution's name. Twenty is too many for one step. So the list has three categories: licences, with eight matters; suspension and cancellation, with seven; and other matters, with five. No category holds more than nine, and each name is one the officers of MoEYS already use.

Here is where the officer meets it. She opens the list of PHEQA's advice that waits for the minister's decision, and she wants only the advice on suspending a full licence. First she picks the category, suspension and cancellation. Seven matters appear, and she picks the one she wants. The list now shows only that advice. She never saw all twenty at once, and she never scrolled past a value she did not need.

## Your turn — Divide a long list into categories

{% hint style="success" %}
**💡 AI usage tip** · Divide a long list into categories

**Bring:** The list of values, and who uses it for what.
**Get:** A list of categories, none holding more than nine values, each with a plain name, with the values that were hard to place marked.
**Time:** ~10–15 min · **Assistant:** any; the tips are tool-neutral.
**Watch for:** The categories are a proposal.
{% endhint %}

**The problem it solves.** A list of more than nine values is about to reach a screen. Before the interaction design is accepted, the analyst needs a first proposal of categories, each with a plain name, to put to the officers who use the list.

Below is a list of values that officers in [your ministry] choose from on a screen, one value per line, with any note on what each means [paste the list]. If more than nine categories are needed, propose a second level above them. Mark any value you could place in two categories, with the two you considered. Output: a list of categories, none holding more than nine values, each with a plain name and the values under it, followed by the values marked as hard to place.

{% tab title="Reading the output" %}
This is the teaching part of the tip. Work through the output the way a chief architect would: what is earned, what is invented, what is missing.

<details>

<summary>1. No step longer than nine</summary>

Four categories, none above six. Each name is plain, and the run marked the three matters it could have placed twice. That is the honest part of the answer: those three are where officers will disagree.

</details>

<details>

<summary>2. A value went missing</summary>

Count the matters: nineteen, not twenty. "Cancelling a provisional licence" and "cancelling a full licence" became one line, "cancelling a licence". The prompt forbade merging, and the list is changed only by its owner. Put both back before the proposal goes anywhere.

</details>

<details>

<summary>3. Let the officers decide where things go</summary>

The run split the suspension matters across two categories. Ask four or five officers where they would look for "ending a suspension" and "setting a cancellation aside". If they look under suspension and cancellation, move them there. In Progressa's example, the officers settled on three categories, of eight, seven and five.

</details>

{% hint style="danger" %}
**Safeguard.** The categories are a proposal. Show them to the officers who use the list and ask each one where they would look for five or six of the values; a value they would look for elsewhere is moved. The list of values itself is changed only by its owner, never by the prompt.
{% endhint %}
