# 1.3 A question with a name on it is part of the specification

{% hint style="info" %}

> **Single message.** An open question with an owner and a date is acceptable; a question answered by a guess is a fault.

## The concept

You are asked to sign a specification. Every line in it looks certain. That is the moment to be careful, because a line that looks certain may be a guess, and nobody can tell a guess from a fact.

In Progressa, a supplier's analyst was writing the part of PHEQA's service in which an institution appeals against a decision of the quality authority. Progressa's regulations give the right to appeal, but the text the analyst had did not say within how long. The analyst needed a number to draw the screens, so the draft said thirty days. Nobody gave that figure. It looks like a fact, and it will be built as a fact: on day thirty-one, the self-service refuses the appeal.

If the true period is longer, an institution with a lawful appeal is turned away by the system, and nobody knows why the system says thirty. The guess may even be right. It is still a fault, and a worse one than a blank would have been, because a blank is visible and a guess is not. Nobody reading the document can tell which of its figures were given and which were invented to keep the work moving.

Now the same line, written under the SDD method. An institution may appeal within the appeal period. The appeal period is to be confirmed by the Registrar of PHEQA by the fifteenth of November. And the document counts it: open question seven of nine. This version is not weaker. It is more complete. It says exactly what is not yet known, who will settle it and by when. The builder builds everything else and leaves the one figure to a setting the Registrar fills in.

Before you sign, ask three questions. First: could the builder work from this document alone, without working anything out and without asking anybody anything, apart from the named open questions? Second: was the review held, with the owner of the document, the builder and the official who will live with the result in the room? Third: is every remaining gap named, owned and counted? When all three answers are yes, the document is good enough to sign.

When you read a draft, look for figures, periods, roles and rules with no source beside them. Ask of each one where it came from. An honest answer is either a source, or a name and a date. And be wary of a draft with no open questions at all. In a new service, that is a warning, not a result: it usually means that somebody answered the hard questions alone.

## Your turn — Find the guessed answers in a draft specification

{% hint style="success" %}
**💡 AI usage tip** · Find the guessed answers in a draft specification

**Bring:** The draft specification and the sources it was written from.
**Get:** A list of the guessed answers in the draft, each turned into a named question with a proposed owner, and the number of open questions the document will carry.
**Time:** ~10–15 min · **Assistant:** any; the tips are tool-neutral.
**Watch for:** The owners are proposals: the manager assigns each question to a person and sets its date.
{% endhint %}

**The problem it solves.** A draft specification mixes figures and rules that somebody gave with figures and rules somebody guessed to keep the work moving. The manager cannot sign it until the guesses are found and each one is turned into a question that a named person will answer.

{% tab title="Reading the output" %}
This is the teaching part of the tip. Work through the output the way a chief architect would: what is earned, what is invented, what is missing.

<details>

<summary>1. The three periods and the panel are caught</summary>

Lines 1 to 3 hold exactly the kind of figure the page warns about: they look certain and nobody gave them. Each comes back as a question in the form the prompt asks for, and the dates are left blank for the manager. The assistant answered none of them.

</details>

<details>

<summary>2. Line 4 points to a source that does not say it</summary>

Article 7 says PHEQA issues a schedule of fees. It does not say what the fee is, and it does not say an appeal carries one. The schedule itself was not pasted. The assistant saw an article number beside the line and treated the whole line as sourced. Both the amount and the rule that an appeal pays the application fee are unproven.

</details>

<details>

<summary>3. The safeguard at work</summary>

The safeguard says to check every line treated as sourced against the source it names. Read against article 7, line 4 fails and becomes a fourth question: whether an appeal carries a fee, and how much, owned by PHEQA's finance officer, who owns the fee. The count rises from 3 to 4. Line 5 passes the same check: article 22 says it word for word.

</details>

<details>

<summary>4. The owners are still proposals</summary>

Every question went to the Registrar. That is a reasonable start, but the time PHEQA takes to decide may rest with whoever sets PHEQA's working rules. The manager assigns each question to a person and sets its date.

</details>

{% hint style="danger" %}
**Safeguard.** The owners are proposals: the manager assigns each question to a person and sets its date. A line the prompt treats as sourced is checked against the source it names before anyone relies on it, because the prompt can point to a source that does not say what it claims.
{% endhint %}
