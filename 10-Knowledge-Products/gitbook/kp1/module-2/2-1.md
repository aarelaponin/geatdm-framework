## The concept

An Enterprise Architecture describes a government in four layers: Business, Data, Application, Technology. For each layer you need three things — the question it answers, the deliverable you produce, and the mistake that catches first-time architects.

Start with the Business layer. The question it answers: what does this body do, for whom, and how well? Not how it is organised inside — what it actually does. The deliverable is a capability map and a service catalogue. A capability is something the body can do — register a learner, run an examination, certify a teacher. A service is how a citizen or another body receives that capability. The mistake first-time architects make is to copy the organisation chart and call it the Business layer. The org chart tells you who reports to whom. It does not tell you what the body does. Two bodies with the same chart can do completely different work. Describe the capabilities, not the boxes.

Second, the Data layer. The question: what information does the body hold, who owns each kind, and where does the authoritative copy live? The deliverable is a data-domain catalogue. A data domain is a kind of information the government agrees on — a person, a learner, a payment — and for each you name the one body that owns the authoritative copy. Data is the longest-lived layer: applications and technology cycle, the data outlasts them. The mistake is to list the databases you can see, one per system — but five systems may each hold their own version of the same learner. The Data layer sits above the databases: agreed domains, one owner each.

Third, the Application layer. The question: what software supports the work, and which capability does each application serve? The deliverable is an application portfolio — every system, mapped to the capability it supports and the data domain it touches. The mistake here is producing a software inventory that lists names and versions but never connects them to what the body does. An inventory tells you that a system exists. A portfolio tells you why it exists, what would break if it failed, and whether two systems are quietly doing the same job. The link up to the Business layer and across to the Data layer is the whole value. Without it, you have an IT asset list, not an architecture.

Fourth, the Technology layer. The question: what does all of this run on? Networks, hosting, the identity platform, the security controls, the data-exchange backbone. The deliverable is a short list of technology standards and a simple infrastructure picture — not a server-by-server audit. The mistake here is the opposite of the others. First-time architects go too deep, too early. They document every server and switch before they understand a single capability. At the architecture level, the Technology layer answers a few questions: what are we standardising on, where are the single points of failure, and what must be running for anything else to work. The detailed audit belongs to the operations team, not the architect.

The four layers are not four separate documents. They connect. Every service traces to a capability in the Business layer. Every capability is served by one or more applications. Every application uses one or more data domains, and both run on the technology layer. When you can trace a citizen-facing service down to the infrastructure it depends on, you are reading the government as a system — which is the whole job. A layer that floats free, with no connection up or down, is the first gap you have found.

**BUSINESS LAYER**

*The question this layer answers:* What does MoEYS do, for whom, and how well?

Interview questions:
1. Which services does a citizen receive from MoEYS directly, and which are delivered by PNEA or a school on its behalf?
2. What can MoEYS do that no other body can — setting the curriculum, funding a school, accrediting a teacher?
3. Which of those capabilities are exercised centrally, and which are delegated to a district?
4. When a parent enrols a child, which body owns each step of that journey?
5. Where does MoEYS's mandate end and PNEA's begin, in writing?
6. Which capability is the Minister's "one learner, one record" promise actually about?

*Deliverable:* a capability map for MoEYS, and a service catalogue naming the citizen-facing services on top of it.

*The mistake to avoid:* copying the MoEYS organisation chart. Directorates are not capabilities. Two ministries with the same chart can do entirely different work.

**DATA LAYER**

*The question this layer answers:* What information does MoEYS hold, who owns each kind, and where is the authoritative copy?

Interview questions:
1. For the learner: which body holds the authoritative record — MoEYS, PNEA, or the planned PLR?
2. The district EMIS uses its own learner numbering and PNEA uses its own candidate numbering. Which is authoritative, and how are they reconciled today?
3. Who owns the school domain — the school census, or something else?
4. What is the legal basis on which MoEYS holds a minor's data, and who signs it off?
5. For each domain you name: one owner, or more than one?
6. Which domains does MoEYS consume rather than own — person identity from PNIA, birth records from Civil Registration?

*Deliverable:* a data-domain catalogue with exactly one named owning body per domain.

*The mistake to avoid:* listing the databases you can see. The district EMIS, the candidate list and the school census are three systems that each hold a version of "learner" — that is one domain with a contested owner, not three domains.

**APPLICATION LAYER**

*The question this layer answers:* What software supports the work, and which capability does each application serve?

Interview questions:
1. District EMIS supports which capability, and which data domains does it touch?
2. What supports enrolment today, between the paper form and the EMIS record?
3. Which applications exchange data with PNEA's candidate management system, and how?
4. Is MoEYS a member of Linkup? If not, how does data leave the ministry?
5. Which systems would stop working if PNIA changed its identity API?
6. Is any application running that no current capability needs?

*Deliverable:* an application portfolio, every system mapped up to a capability and across to its data domains.

*The mistake to avoid:* producing a software inventory. A list of names and versions tells you a system exists; a portfolio tells you why it exists and what breaks without it.

**TECHNOLOGY LAYER**

*The question this layer answers:* What does all of this run on?

Interview questions:
1. What is MoEYS standardising on for hosting, and is that a decision or an accident?
2. Which shared platforms does the sector depend on — Linkup, PNIA e-KYC, PayPro?
3. Where are the single points of failure between a school and the EMIS?
4. What connectivity can a rural school actually rely on, and what does that force offline?
5. Who operates what: MoEYS, PDGA, or a supplier?

*Deliverable:* a short list of technology standards in use, and a one-page infrastructure picture.

*The mistake to avoid:* going too deep, too early. A server-by-server audit belongs to operations. At architecture level you need the standards, the dependencies and the failure points.

{% tab title="Reading the output" %}
This is the teaching part of the play. Work through the output the way a chief architect would — what is earned, what is invented, what is missing.

<details>

<summary>1. The Data-layer mistake note is the best line in the template</summary>

The prompt asked for "the one mistake to avoid" per layer. For Data the model did not give the generic answer; it used the input — three systems each numbering learners their own way — and turned it into the specific warning that this is *one contested domain, not three domains*. That is the finding the whole of Module 2 is pointing at, produced before a single interview.

</details>

<details>

<summary>2. It quietly resolved a question the input leaves open</summary>

Data question 1 asks whether the authoritative learner record sits with MoEYS, PNEA "or the planned PLR". The input says the PLR does not exist. Asking who owns a domain that no body currently holds is the right question, but the template does not say the honest answer — *nobody owns it today* — so an interviewer could record a target-state answer as a fact. Add that to the question before you use it.

</details>

<details>

<summary>3. What is missing: the body that should have been asked about and was not</summary>

The input ends with "there is no data-standards function in the education sector." That absence is the reason the three learner numberings were never reconciled, and no question in any layer asks about it. Add one to the Business layer: *who is accountable for a data standard across MoEYS, PNEA and the PLR, and if nobody is, who should be?*

</details>

<details>

<summary>4. The safeguard applies, and it is not a formality</summary>

The safeguard says the template is a starting structure for capture, not the architecture. Every "deliverable" line here describes something you do not have yet. Read as a capture plan it is good; read as a description of Progressa's education sector it would be entirely invented, because no interview has happened. That distinction is the difference between A9 and A13.

</details>

{% hint style="danger" %}
**Safeguard for this play.** The template is a starting structure for capture, not the architecture itself. The authoritative layer descriptions come from the body's documents and interviews — the template tells you what to ask, not what the answer is.
{% endhint %}
