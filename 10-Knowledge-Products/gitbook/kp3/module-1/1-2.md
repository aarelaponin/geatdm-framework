> **Single message.** One page with five domains, a foundation of governance, policy and law carrying access, digital data, interoperability and digital identity, shows your minister what the country has and what it lacks.

## The concept

Ask a ministry what the country has for digital government, and you often get a list of projects, each with its own donor. A list hides what is missing. One page with five domains shows your minister both.

PAERA, the GovStack reference architecture, gives the picture. It says that a country's digital governance infrastructure has two parts. The first is a foundation framework of governance, policy and legal components. The second is four pillars: Access, Digital Data, Interoperability and Digital Identity. Services, such as registering a learner or paying a scholarship, stand on the pillars. PAERA also explains why the pillars come early. They are the technical conditions that services need, and a country should address them before it pursues wide and ambitious plans.

The method reads the foundation as a fifth domain beside the four pillars. That reading is this course's own. Its reason is practical: a pillar without a law, a budget and a body that decides is not yet infrastructure. So there are five domains, each with a short code. GOV is governance and policy, with the legal framework. ACC is access: connections, devices, skills, and help for people who need it. DAT is digital data, with the state registers. INT is interoperability, the exchange of data between systems. IDN is digital identity.

Here is the map for Progressa, the fictional country of these examples. It is step one of the method, and the first output of the assessment: one page that fixes its scope. For each domain it names the body that answers. For digital data, that is the statistics unit of the ministry of education, which runs PEMIS, its school information system, with PNEA, the examination authority. For interoperability, it is PDGA, the digital government authority, which operates Linkup, the national data exchange. The page also records what is there and what is not. Linkup runs as a pilot. PLR, the learner registry, holds records for some learners only, and is not yet the authoritative learner register.

Why one page? PAERA describes enterprise architecture as documents that look at an organisation from business and IT together. Their purpose is to close the gap in communication between the two. The five-domain map does the same for a country's infrastructure. It gives the policy side and the technical side one shared language. The minister reads the gaps in law, money and ownership. The architect reads the gaps in systems and data. When they decide what comes first, both point to the same part of the same page. A list of projects cannot do this, because it shows only what someone chose to fund.

## Your turn — Place your systems, laws and public bodies on the five-domain map

{% hint style="success" %}
**💡 AI usage tip** · Place your systems, laws and public bodies on the five-domain map

**Bring:** A list of systems, laws and public bodies, one line each.
**Get:** A map of the five domains with every item placed and its reasoning, a list of the items not placed or placed twice, and what seems to be missing in each domain.
**Time:** ~10–15 min · **Assistant:** any; the tips are tool-neutral.
**Watch for:** An item that fits two domains is discussed with the body that owns it and is not forced into one.
{% endhint %}

**The problem it solves.** Before an assessment starts, the team needs one page that shows what the country has in each domain and who answers for it. This prompt places a list of systems, laws and public bodies on the five domains and lists what it could not place, so that the gaps and the doubtful items are visible.

{% tabs %}
{% tab title="Prompt" %}
Copy the prompt, replace the bracketed parts with your own context, and run it in the AI assistant of your choice. Never paste personal data or unpublished documents — see [How to use the plays](../../start-here/how-to-use-the-plays.md).

```text
Below is a list of the systems, laws and public bodies that bear on digital public services in [country X], with one line on each [paste the list]. Place each item on one of five domains: GOV — governance and policy, with the legal framework; ACC — access (connectivity, devices and channels, skills, assisted service, inclusion); DAT — digital data (data policy, state registers, data quality, sharing, protection, statistics); INT — interoperability (the data exchange, its members, its standards, the catalogue of services, shared building blocks); IDN — digital identity (coverage, sign-in, use by services, identity for children, legal validity and e-signature). For each item give its domain and one line of reasoning. Where an item could belong to two domains, name both and do not choose. Then, for each domain, list what you would expect to find and did not. Output: a map of the five domains with every item placed and its reasoning, a list of the items you could not place or that fit two domains, and a list of what seems to be missing in each domain.
```

{% tab title="Reading the output" %}
This is the teaching part of the tip. Work through the output the way a chief architect would: what is earned, what is invented, what is missing.

<details>

<summary>1. The map follows the frame</summary>

Every item lands where the frame page of E1 puts it, with PayPro under INT as the payment rail and PNEA's records under DAT. Leaving PLR in two domains is right: E1 assigns DAT to the statistics unit with PNEA, and INT to PDGA, so both must be asked about it.

</details>

<details>

<summary>2. "Not found" means "not on the list"</summary>

The third column reads like findings, but it only says what a 13-line list did not mention. Some entries are true for Progressa; the assistant could not know that from the list. Treat the column as questions for the desk assessment, not as gaps.

</details>

<details>

<summary>3. Settle the double placements with their owners</summary>

Put PLR to the MoEYS statistics unit and to PDGA together: what does it hold, under what law, and who calls it? The answer decides which questionnaire asks about it first.

</details>

{% hint style="danger" %}
**Safeguard.** An item that fits two domains is discussed with the body that owns it and is not forced into one. The owner knows what the item does in practice; a model that chooses for it hides a question the assessment should ask.
{% endhint %}
