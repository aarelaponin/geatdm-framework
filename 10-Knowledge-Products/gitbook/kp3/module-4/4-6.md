# 4.6 Reuse is the return on planning

{% hint style="info" %}
🎬 **Video in production:** *Reuse is the return on planning* (~4 min).
The page below does not depend on the video: it carries what the video will say. The [video index](../../start-here/video-index.md) is the page to watch for links.
{% endhint %}

> **Single message.** Learner registration reuses the identity block and the scholarship payment reuses the Payments block, a saving visible only to someone who plans for the whole government, because inside one project building your own looks quicker.

## The concept

Inside a single project, building your own identity check or your own payment link often looks quicker than waiting for a shared one. Seen from the whole government, it is the more expensive choice.

Take two services in Progressa. Learner registration needs to know who the applicant is, so it reuses the identity block: one sign-in, one identifier for the service, and no identity system of its own. The scholarship payment needs to pay learners, so it reuses the Payments block: one account mapper, and one route through the payer bank to the payment systems the country already runs. Neither service builds what the country already has.

Alone, the registration service would need its own enrolment, credentials, security and support staff, which are the costs of an identity system. Alone, the scholarship service would need its own links to each bank and mobile money provider, and every learner's account details in its own register. With the shared blocks, one verification service is used by many, and the account mapper keeps payment details out of each programme's register. The identity service can serve other blocks, public services and private services alike.

Education is not a stretch for these blocks. The Payments specification itself names the payment of school fees, a group of vouchers that can be redeemed only at schools, and conditional transfers for school fee payment. The Registration specification asks that several registrations can be combined in one service, so that an applicant fills in one form instead of several. Each of these can run on the same two blocks.

So why does the saving so often go unclaimed? Each project is funded to deliver its own service on its own date, and inside the project building its own looks quicker. The first ministry pays for a shared block, and the second, third and fourth reuse it. That sum exists only at the level of the whole government. PAERA asks planners to identify shared services, workflows and data that meet the needs of several agencies, and its principles of whole-of-government and once-only point the same way. A table of which services reuse which block gives the business side and IT one shared language for that decision.


## Your turn — Count the services that would reuse each shared block

{% hint style="success" %}
**💡 AI usage tip** · Count the services that would reuse each shared block

**Bring:** The inventory of services with their owners and needs, and the list of shared blocks.
**Get:** A count, for each shared block, of the services that would reuse it, confirmed by their owners, as the first column of the table of what to fund first.
**Time:** ~10–15 min · **Assistant:** any; the tips are tool-neutral.
**Watch for:** A service is counted only when its owner confirms it would use the block.
{% endhint %}

**The problem it solves.** The return on a shared block is the number of services that use it, and a finance ministry will ask for that number. This prompt takes the inventory of services and counts, for each shared block, the services that would reuse it. The count is the first column of the table of what to fund first.

For each shared block, count the services that would reuse it, and list them by name and owner. Mark a service 'confirmed by its owner' only where the inventory says its owner has agreed, and mark every other service 'to confirm'. Output: a count, for each shared block, of the services that would reuse it, confirmed by their owners, as a table with one row for each block, ready to become the first column of the table of what to fund first.

{% tab title="Reading the output" %}
This is the teaching part of the tip. Work through the output the way a chief architect would: what is earned, what is invented, what is missing.

<details>

<summary>1. The register's count matches the plan</summary>

Four services on the learner register is the same count the table of what to fund first uses, and the Payments block's single service is the scholarship pilot. Both come straight from the inventory.

</details>

<details>

<summary>2. Only one service is confirmed</summary>

Of the twelve uses counted, four rest on an owner's agreement, all from the same service. Presented as a count of four, the register's row overstates what is known. The finance ministry should see the "confirmed" column first.

</details>

<details>

<summary>3. The scholarship's need for identity is an assumption</summary>

The inventory says the scholarship pilot needs to know who the person is. It may instead rely on learners already verified in PLR, and never sign anyone in. Ask MoEYS and the Ministry of Finance; if so, the identity block's count falls to one.

</details>

{% hint style="danger" %}
**Safeguard.** A service is counted only when its owner confirms it would use the block. A count that includes services whose owners were never asked overstates the return on reuse, and the finance ministry will find the gap.
{% endhint %}
