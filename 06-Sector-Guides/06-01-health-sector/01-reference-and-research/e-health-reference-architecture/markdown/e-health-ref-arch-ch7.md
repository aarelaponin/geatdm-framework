# SECTION 7: CAPACITY BUILDING & TRAINING

## 7.0 Introduction

Capacity building represents one of the most critical success factors for sustainable e-health ecosystem implementation. Technical infrastructure and legal frameworks alone cannot ensure adoption and effective use—human capacity at all levels determines whether digital health transformation achieves its objectives.
This section addresses the comprehensive capacity building requirements for national e-health ecosystems, drawing on lessons from Estonia's gradual capacity development over three decades and Progressa's intensive planned capacity building approach. The framework recognizes that capacity building must occur simultaneously across multiple stakeholder groups: healthcare professionals, technical staff, administrative personnel, and patients themselves.
Key Principles:
Continuous Learning: Capacity building is not a one-time activity but an ongoing process
Multi-Level Approach: Address individual, organizational, and systemic capacity simultaneously
Practical Focus: Emphasize hands-on, practice-based learning over theoretical knowledge
Contextualization: Adapt training to local language, culture, and practice patterns
Sustainability: Build train-the-trainer capacity to ensure long-term knowledge transfer
Measurement: Track competency development and link training to performance outcomes

## 7.2 Training Programs

### 7.2.1 Training Curriculum Architecture

Modular Design Principle: Training programs should be designed as modular components that can be combined based on role, competency level, and organizational context.
```
Training Curriculum Structure
├── Foundation Modules (e-health ecosystem overview, legal/ethical basics)
├── Role-Specific Modules (clinical, technical, administrative)
├── System-Specific Modules (EHR, ePrescription, patient portal)
├── Advanced Modules (analytics, AI/ML, innovation)
└── Refresher Modules (updates, new features, best practices)
```

Learning Objectives Framework (Bloom's Taxonomy):
Knowledge: Recall facts, concepts, standards
Comprehension: Explain, describe, interpret
Application: Use knowledge in new situations, operate systems
Analysis: Break down information, troubleshoot problems
Synthesis: Create new workflows, design solutions
Evaluation: Assess quality, make informed decisions

## 7.3 Training Delivery Models

### 7.3.1 Delivery Modalities

In-Person Classroom Training
Best for: Hands-on technical training, clinician training with practice, interactive workshops
Advantages: Direct interaction, immediate feedback, networking, hands-on practice
Challenges: Scheduling conflicts, geographic barriers, higher cost, limited scalability
Implementation Tips: 
Use healthcare facilities' training rooms
Provide computers/tablets for hands-on exercises
Include real clinical scenarios from local context
Keep groups small (15-20 for classroom, 8-12 for hands-on)
Online/E-Learning
Best for: Foundation knowledge, refresher training, asynchronous learning, wide geographic reach
Advantages: Self-paced, scalable, cost-effective, reusable, accessibility
Challenges: Requires digital literacy, less interaction, requires self-motivation, hands-on limitations
Implementation Tips: 
Use learning management system (LMS)
Include video lectures (10-15 minutes each)
Add interactive quizzes and assessments
Provide downloadable resources
Offer multiple languages and subtitles
Ensure mobile-friendly design
Include discussion forums
Blended Learning
Best for: Most comprehensive training programs
Approach: Combine online self-paced modules with in-person workshops
Advantages: Balances knowledge acquisition with hands-on practice, efficient use of training time
Example Flow: 
Online pre-work: Foundation concepts, system overview
In-person workshop: Hands-on practice, complex scenarios, Q&A
Online post-work: Reinforcement, assessment, additional resources
Webinars and Virtual Workshops
Best for: Updates, demonstrations, national reach, international expert access
Advantages: Real-time interaction, cost-effective, recordable, wide reach
Challenges: Technology barriers, time zone challenges, engagement difficulty
Implementation Tips: 
Use reliable platforms (Zoom, Teams, WebEx)
Include polls and Q&A
Record for later viewing
Limit to 60-90 minutes
Provide materials in advance
On-the-Job Training and Mentoring
Best for: System go-live support, complex workflows, continuous learning

Supervisor assessments
Error rates and near-miss reports
Indicators: 
Increased system adoption and usage
Improved data quality and completeness
Reduced medication errors
Better workflow efficiency
Fewer support tickets for basic issues
Target: ≥70% of trainees demonstrating desired behaviors
Level 4: Results (6-12 Months Post-Training)
Measures: Organizational impact and outcomes
Methods: KPI tracking, ROI analysis, outcome studies
Metrics: 
Clinical quality improvements (reduced medication errors, better outcomes)
Operational efficiency (reduced documentation time, faster order processing)

## 7.10 Common Pitfalls and How to Avoid Them

### 7.10.1 Insufficient Investment in Training

Pitfall: Viewing training as discretionary expense that can be cut when budgets are tight. Minimal, superficial training leads to poor adoption, errors, and implementation failure.
Avoidance Strategies:
Build training into business case with demonstrated ROI
Benchmark training investment (10-20% of implementation budget)
Show correlation between training investment and adoption success
Make training non-negotiable component of implementation
Secure multi-year funding commitment
### 7.10.2 One-Size-Fits-All Training

Pitfall: Generic training that doesn't address specific needs of different roles, settings, or specialties. Results in irrelevant content, wasted time, and poor engagement.
Avoidance Strategies:
Conduct thorough training needs assessment by role
Develop modular, role-specific training curricula
Customize examples and scenarios for different contexts
Allow flexibility for local adaptation
Focus on "need to know" not "nice to know"
### 7.10.3 Training Too Early or Too Late

Pitfall: Training months before go-live leads to skill decay. Training days before go-live creates panic and insufficient competency.
Avoidance Strategies:
Optimal timing: 2-4 weeks before go-live for core training
Provide refresher training immediately before go-live
Just-in-time microlearning for specific features
Phased training aligned with phased implementation
Ongoing training for new features and updates
### 7.10.4 Lack of Hands-On Practice

Pitfall: Lecture-heavy, demonstration-only training without opportunity for learners to practice. Results in inability to perform tasks independently post-training.
Avoidance Strategies:
Minimum 50% hands-on practice time in all training
Realistic simulation environments with test data
Guided practice followed by independent practice
Problem-solving scenarios that reflect real workflows
Assessment through practical demonstration, not just written tests
### 7.10.5 No Post-Training Support

Pitfall: Training ends at classroom exit. No support when users encounter real-world challenges. Leads to frustration, workarounds, and system abandonment.
Avoidance Strategies:
Super-user networks providing at-the-elbow support
Helpdesk with extended hours during go-live period
Quick reference guides and job aids available at point of care
Online knowledge base and FAQs
Regular follow-up training sessions (1 month, 3 months post-go-live)
Communities of practice for ongoing peer support
### 7.10.6 Underestimating Change Management Needs

Pitfall: Focusing solely on technical skills training, ignoring emotional and cultural aspects of change. Users know "how" but not "why," leading to resistance and minimal adoption.
Avoidance Strategies:
Integrate change management into training (vision, benefits, addressing fears)
Communicate extensively before, during, and after training
Involve clinical leaders and champions
Address concerns openly and provide reassurance
Celebrate successes and early wins
Provide incentives and recognition for adoption
### 7.10.7 Poor Quality Training Materials

Pitfall: Materials developed hastily, poorly translated, full of errors, or not updated as system changes. Erodes credibility and confuses learners.
Avoidance Strategies:
Allocate adequate time and budget for professional material development
Pilot test materials and incorporate feedback
Review by subject matter experts for accuracy
Professional translation by healthcare-knowledgeable translators
Version control and regular update cycles
Quality assurance process for all materials
### 7.10.8 Trainer Burnout

Pitfall: Too few trainers delivering too many sessions. Trainers become exhausted, quality declines, trainers leave.
Avoidance Strategies:
Train sufficient number of trainers (ratio of 1 trainer per 30-50 trainees)
Rotate trainers to prevent burnout
Provide trainers with adequate preparation time between sessions
Offer incentives, recognition, and career development for trainers
Build trainer community for peer support
Plan realistic training schedules with buffer time
### 7.10.9 No Measurement of Training Effectiveness

Pitfall: Training delivery becomes "check the box" activity. No tracking of competency, adoption, or outcomes. Unable to improve or demonstrate value.
Avoidance Strategies:
Define clear training success metrics (Kirkpatrick Levels 1-4)
Conduct competency assessments, not just attendance tracking
Link training to system usage analytics
Track quality and error metrics post-training
Regular evaluation and continuous improvement
Report training effectiveness to leadership
### 7.10.10 Over-Reliance on External Vendors

Pitfall: All training delivered by vendors or external consultants. No internal capacity built. When contract ends, no one can maintain or expand training.
Avoidance Strategies:
Mandatory knowledge transfer clauses in vendor contracts
Train-the-trainer approach from day one
Build internal training team and infrastructure
Document all training materials and make them editable
Gradual transition from vendor-led to internally-led training
Retain institutional knowledge through communities of practice and documentation

## 7.11 Lessons Learned from Estonia and Progressa

### 7.11.1 Estonian Experience: Incremental Capacity Building

Context: Estonia built e-health capacity gradually over 20+ years alongside system evolution.
Key Lessons:
1. Start Early with Digital Literacy
Estonia invested in national digital literacy from 1990s (Tiger Leap program)
Healthcare professionals grew up with digital tools
Foundation of digital skills accelerated e-health adoption
Implication: Countries with low digital literacy must invest in broad digital skills training before or parallel to e-health system training.
2. Leverage National Infrastructure
Estonian e-health training leveraged existing national digital ID infrastructure
Healthcare professionals already familiar with ID-card authentication
Training could focus on health-specific applications, not basic digital skills
Implication: Coordinate e-health training with broader digital government initiatives to avoid duplicating foundational training.
3. Continuous Evolution Requires Continuous Training
Estonia's system evolved from document-based to event-based architecture (upTIS)
Required retraining and upskilling of existing workforce
Established culture of continuous learning as expectation
Implication: Build training infrastructure for long-term, not just initial implementation. Plan for regular updates and re-training as system evolves.
4. Patient Training is Critical for Adoption
Estonia's high patient portal adoption (>80%) correlates with extensive patient education
Multiple channels: websites, videos, in-person demos, media campaigns
Emphasis on patient rights and transparency (audit logs)
Implication: Invest equally in patient-facing training and education, not just healthcare professionals. Patient adoption drives provider adoption.
5. Super-User Model for Sustainability
Estonia identified and trained super-users at each healthcare facility
Super-users provide first-line support to colleagues
Reduces dependency on central helpdesk
Creates facility-level champions
Implication: Train and empower super-users as core sustainability strategy.
### 7.11.2 Progressa Experience: Intensive Capacity Building for Transformation

Context: Progressa is building e-health capacity from ground up with comprehensive, planned approach.
Key Lessons:
1. Comprehensive Training Needs Assessment
Progressa DHP project conducted detailed skills gap analysis
Identified needs across technical, clinical, administrative, and governance roles
Quantified training requirements (number of trainees, topics, duration)
Informed realistic budget and timeline
Implication: Don't skip needs assessment. Understand current capacity and gap to target before designing training program.
2. Standards-First Training Approach
Progressa prioritized training on international standards (FHIR, SNOMED CT, LOINC, ICD-11)
Built understanding of standards before system-specific training
Enables flexibility and reduces vendor lock-in
Implication: For greenfield implementations, invest in standards education early. Creates foundation for interoperability and sustainability.
3. Multilingual Context Complexity
Progressa must deliver training in the local language and English
Terminology translation and localization is major undertaking
Requires specialized translators with healthcare knowledge
Implication: In multilingual contexts, allocate significant time and budget for translation and localization. Use professional translators, not just bilingual staff.
4. Intensive Train-the-Trainer Investment
Progressa project allocated substantial resources to developing national trainer capacity
International experts worked closely with local trainers
Created comprehensive trainer materials and support systems
Implication: In countries with limited health informatics expertise, invest heavily upfront in train-the-trainer to build sustainable training capacity.
5. Integration with Legal and Regulatory Capacity
Progressa training program includes legal and regulatory dimensions
Training for policymakers, lawyers, data protection officers
Parallel capacity building in governance and legal frameworks
Implication: Capacity building extends beyond technical and clinical domains. Include legal, governance, and policy capacity building.
6. Phased Training Aligned with Implementation
Progressa training phased to align with DHP Core → MIS Integration → PACS → BI rollout
Avoids training too early (skill decay)
Allows learning from each phase to inform next phase training
Implication: Sequence training carefully with implementation phases. Don't try to train everyone on everything at once.
7. Academic Partnership for Long-Term Capacity
Progressa project includes development of health informatics curriculum in universities
Creates pipeline of qualified graduates for long-term
Reduces dependency on external expertise over time
Implication: Partner with academic institutions early to establish sustainable workforce pipeline.

### 7.12.3 Universal Recommendations

Regardless of maturity level, all countries should:
Invest adequately in training (10-20% of implementation budget)
Prioritize train-the-trainer for sustainability
Take role-based, practical, hands-on approach to training
Provide ongoing support post-training (super-users, helpdesk, communities)
Measure training effectiveness rigorously (all four Kirkpatrick levels)
Build long-term capacity (academic partnerships, centers of excellence)
Foster continuous learning culture (not one-time event)
Adapt to local context (language, culture, workflows, constraints)
Integrate training with implementation (timing, sequencing, support)
Learn from others (Estonia, Progressa, other leading implementations)
